# 📍 Find your Level

!!! who "What this page is for"
    There is no single path that works from beginning to end. **What you should be paying attention to depends on how much you already have.**
    This page helps you find yourself on the wealth ladder, and shows **where your effort is best spent at that stage**. Read it first, then pick the chapters that fit you.

---

## Why split things into Levels?

A lot of beginners fixate on returns and keep asking "which one goes up fastest". But here's the reality:

> **At different stages of wealth, completely different things decide whether you get richer.**

- When you have a few thousand dollars, an extra 2% of return is a few dollars — **what actually moves the needle is your income and your savings rate**.
- Once your assets are large enough, your salary can't keep up with them, and only then do **asset allocation, tax efficiency and withdrawal strategy start to run the show**.

So instead of asking "which one goes up fastest", ask first: **which level am I on, and what should I be doing at this level?**

This "levels of wealth" idea is adapted from data scientist Nick Maggiulli's *The Wealth Ladder*. What follows is my own reorganisation of it, from the point of view of **long-term investing**.

---

## The wealth ladder: five levels, and what to focus on

The levels below are based on **investable net worth** — your assets minus your debts, **not counting the home you live in** (you live in it; you can't withdraw living expenses from it).

!!! tip "Read this first"
    - These numbers are **reference lines**, and they drift upward with inflation. What matters is **which stage you're in and what you should be focusing on**, not landing exactly on a threshold.
    - Amounts are in **US dollars, as illustration**. If you live elsewhere, don't convert at the exchange rate — scale them to your own cost of living instead. The ratios are what carry the meaning.
    - Going down the ladder, your "wealth engine" **shifts from *you* (earning a salary) to *money* (assets compounding on their own)**.
    - Most people spend their whole lives somewhere in **L2–L4**. That is completely normal.

<div class="md-typeset" markdown>

| Level | Investable net worth (approx., USD) | Main engine | What to focus on at this level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **🔴 L1 Survival** | In debt / under $10k | Your income | Clear high-interest debt, build 3–6 months of emergency cash, start learning the concepts. **No rush to invest yet.** |
| **🟢 L2 Starting out** | $10k – $100k | Income + savings rate | Push up income and savings rate, open your **first total-market portfolio**, build the habit of investing on a fixed schedule. |
| **🟡 L3 Middle class** | $100k – $1M | Savings + assets starting to pull | Keep the discipline, consider buying the home you live in (cheap leverage + somewhere to live), get your allocation right. |
| **🔵 L4 Affluent** | $1M – $10M | Asset returns lead | Risk management, tax efficiency, seriously running the retirement numbers — independence stops being hypothetical here. |
| **⚪ L5+ Freedom** | Over $10M | Assets | Capital preservation, withdrawal strategy, estate planning; designing the retirement you actually want. |

</div>

## A bit more on each level

### 🔴 L1 Survival: stop the bleeding before you think about investing
Still carrying high-interest credit card or personal loan debt? Then **paying it off is the highest-return "investment" available to you** (clearing a card charging 20% a year is a guaranteed 20% — no stock will promise you that). After that, build a cash buffer so a surprise doesn't force you to sell assets or borrow again. At this level, **understanding the concepts is enough; there's no rush to get in**.

### 🟢 L2 Starting out: the habit matters more than picking the right thing
The biggest lever at this level is **you**: raises, changing jobs, a side income, keeping your cost of living under control. At the same time, open the simplest possible total-market portfolio and buy into it **on a fixed schedule**, training yourself to keep buying through a downturn instead of reacting. The amount doesn't matter. **The habit is what's valuable.**

### 🟡 L3 Middle class: discipline, time, and somewhere to live
Your savings start to show results and compounding begins to bite. The job at this level isn't to chase higher returns — it's to **not wreck it**: follow the plan, rebalance on schedule, and don't rebuild your portfolio because of a news headline or FOMO.

This is also the stage where **buying the home you live in** comes into play. A mortgage on your own home is, for most people, **the cheapest and most useful leverage they will ever have access to**: the interest rate is far below any margin loan or leveraged product, and it also **solves the very real need to have a roof over your head**. Terms — deposit size, rates, length — differ enormously by country, so check what applies where you live. (We'll unpack the principle in the intermediate section.)

### 🔵 L4 Affluent: from "earning a lot" to "keeping it well"
Your assets are now large enough that their swings outrun your salary, so this is the level where **tax, fees, withdrawals and risk management** start to matter. Financial independence comes into view here — this is the point to seriously run the "how much do I need to retire" numbers (see the Retirement section). You can look into factor investing if you want depth, but **it isn't required**; a simple total-market portfolio takes you a very long way.

### ⚪ L5+ Freedom: designing the life you want
By this level the question is no longer "how do I make more" but "**how do I spend it without anxiety, and pass it on**". The focus shifts to capital preservation, withdrawal strategy and estate planning. Money has stopped being the goal and become a tool.

## The other axis: your allocation path by age

Levels tell you **where your net worth is**; but **where the money actually sits** follows a second thread, tied to **age**. This site recommends one path that is **simple, low-cost, and needs no complicated products**:

<div class="md-typeset" markdown>

| Stage | Allocation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **0 – 20 years old** | **VOO** (US large-cap) | Young, longest runway, able to absorb volatility — you can lean into the market with the strongest growth potential. |
| **20 – 30** | **Shift gradually from VOO to VT** | Your assets are getting real, and betting on a single country stops making sense. Move to global diversification over this decade. |
| **30 onward, for life** | **VT (global equity), hold it** | The whole world in one purchase: the least fuss, the most diversification. **No switch to bonds, no fiddling.** |

</div>

!!! example "Why hold VT even in retirement? — one important study"
    Conventional wisdom says the older you get, the more bonds you should hold. But a large study, **"Beyond the Status Quo: A Critical Assessment of Lifecycle Investment Advice"** (Anarkulova, Cederburg & O'Doherty), simulated using a century of data across 38 countries and reached a counterintuitive conclusion:

    > **Holding globally diversified equity for life (roughly one-third domestic, two-thirds international; the first version used half and half, with the same result) beat the traditional "add bonds as you age" approach over the long run — on retirement wealth, on sustainable withdrawals, and even on what's left for your heirs.**

    Bonds look safe, but their long-run returns are too low, which actually makes running out of money *more* likely. That's why we **hold VT from 30 onward and keep holding it through retirement**. VT is global equity by construction, which is exactly what the study is describing.

!!! warning "But — only if you can actually hold on"
    The price of being all-equity is **volatility** (drawdowns can get ugly). This only works if you **won't panic-sell in a bear market**.
    If you know your stomach isn't that strong, you can review your real risk tolerance in the **years before retirement** and decide whether a little in bonds is worth it for peace of mind. But be clear about this: **the historical data says the people who can hold on usually win.**

!!! warning "The honest truth about leverage"
    You may have heard that young people should invest with leverage. **This site will not recommend leveraged ETFs (LETFs), options or margin.** They're expensive, volatile and hard to hold; over the long run they very rarely beat simply owning VT and leaving it alone, and they cost you both time and money.

    The one form of cheap leverage worth using is **the mortgage on your own home at L3**: low rate, and it solves your housing need at the same time. Beyond that, **the simple age path (VOO → VT) will take you a very long way.**
