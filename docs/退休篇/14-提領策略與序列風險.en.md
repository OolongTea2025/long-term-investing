# Chapter 14. Withdrawal strategy & sequence risk — the killer behind that 5% failure rate

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 3–5 · retirement planning (essential if you're near or in retirement)**<span class="lvtag">L3–L5</span>
    The last chapter said the 4% rule succeeds about 95% of the time — and almost all of that 5% of failures die from the same cause: **sequence risk**. This chapter explains why the first few years of retirement are the most dangerous of your life, and gives seven ways to push the success rate back up.

!!! tip "The short version"
    **In the accumulation phase what matters is the average return; in retirement what matters is the order of returns.** If a severe bear market hits in your first few years of retirement while you're selling to live on, your capital gets squeezed by falling prices *and* withdrawals at the same time, and never recovers. That's **sequence risk**. The answers: **keep a cash or bond buffer, be flexible about withdrawals, delay retirement by a year or two if you must, or use a lower withdrawal rate.**

---

## 1. Same average return, wildly different endings — why?

First, let's kill the biggest misconception: **"as long as the long-run average return is good enough, retirement is safe."** Wrong.

!!! example "Retiree A gets lucky, Retiree B doesn't — identical average returns, opposite fates"
    A and B have the same retirement savings, withdraw the same amount each year, and get **exactly the same average return over 30 years**. The only difference is **which year the crash arrives**:

    - **A (lucky)**: the first few years are **calm or rising**, and the crash comes much later.
    - **B (unlucky)**: **a crash lands in the first few years**, and the recovery comes afterwards.

    The result? **B may run out of money before the retirement period is over, while A is fine and even leaves a substantial estate.**

    Identical average returns, completely different fates — and the difference is **the order** of those returns.

That's sequence risk. In the **accumulation phase** the order doesn't matter (you aren't selling, so a fall and recovery costs you nothing, and a downturn actually lets you buy cheaply). But in the **withdrawal phase** the order becomes a matter of survival.

## 2. Why are the first few years of retirement so lethal?

!!! danger "Falling prices × withdrawals = a double hit"
    After you retire, **you no longer have a salary and have to sell assets to live**. If a downturn arrives just as you retire:

    1. Your portfolio has **already shrunk** (say by 40%).
    2. You **still have to sell** to fund living costs — which means **selling more shares at the low**.
    3. Those shares **never participate in the recovery that follows**.

    Capital gets eroded by the fall and the selling together, and once it drops below a certain point, **you can't recover even when the market rises again** — because you no longer have enough shares to multiply by that gain.

Which echoes Howard Marks in Chapter 9: **"being forced to sell at the bottom" is precisely the moment temporary volatility becomes permanent loss.** Retirement withdrawals are the scenario most likely to trigger it.

!!! note "The same idea: a downturn means the opposite in accumulation and withdrawal"
    - **Accumulation**: a downturn = **a discount**; your fixed contributions buy more shares → it's a friend.
    - **Withdrawal**: a downturn = **a double hit**; you're forced to sell low → it's an enemy.

    So as you move from accumulating to withdrawing, both your mindset and your strategy have to change with it.

## 3. Seven ways to push the success rate back up

The good news is that sequence risk **can be managed**. Here are seven measures you can combine.

!!! success "1. Keep a cash or bond buffer (the most important one)"
    Around retirement, hold **2–3 years of living expenses** in cash or short-dated bonds.

    - In a downturn, **spend the buffer instead of selling equities** → give the market time to recover.
    - This is **the real role of bonds in a retirement portfolio** (see Chapter 11): not to chase return, but to be the cushion that stops you selling cheaply.

!!! success "2. Flexible withdrawals (spend less in bad years)"
    Don't rigidly take 4% no matter what. **In down years, actively cut non-essential spending** (less travel, delay replacing the car).

    - Sell less, keep more capital.
    - Both research and practice show: **people willing to adjust spending flexibly have a far lower probability of running out.**

!!! success "3. Crash right before retirement → delay a year or two"
    If **a big fall arrives just as you're about to retire**, the simplest and most effective response is to **retire a year or two later, or do some part-time work for a year or two afterwards** to top up. Avoiding the worst possible moment to start withdrawing has a startling effect.

!!! success "4. Use a more conservative withdrawal rate"
    Going from 4% to **3.5% or 3%** raises the success rate substantially. The cost is that you need a larger pile (see the multiplier table in Chapter 13). **Safety versus retiring early — you choose.**

!!! success "5. If you want a bond cushion — build it over the last ten years"
    This applies only if section 4 below has convinced you that you need a cushion. Don't switch from all-equity to a balanced portfolio overnight on the day you retire. **Adjust gradually along a glidepath over about ten years beforehand**, so that by the time you enter retirement, the volatility is already at a level you can carry.

!!! success "6. Know what the 4% research assumed (roughly 1:1 stocks and bonds)"
    The original 4% rule research is based on a portfolio of roughly **1:1 stocks and bonds**. If you hold VT for life, you're running a more aggressive mix than the research did — the median outcome is usually better, but the falls along the way are deeper, which makes moves 1 and 2 (a cash buffer, flexible withdrawals) matter even more for you. (Section 4 below covers this.)

!!! success "7. Keep fees down"
    Fees are pure leakage that **takes a bite every single year**. Choose low-cost ETFs; every basis point you save is margin of safety over the long run.

## 4. "100% stocks for life" vs "add bonds in retirement" — how do you choose?

You might well ask: didn't Chapter 8 say the Cederburg paper proves **VT for life with no bonds** is optimal? So why does this chapter tell you to keep a bond buffer?

The tension is real and worth laying out plainly:

!!! quote "They don't actually contradict — it depends on whether you can hold on"
    - **The Cederburg paper is about what's optimal on average**: across a vast number of simulations, all-equity for life produced the best **median** outcome (most wealth, and even a lower probability of ruin).
    - **But "optimal on average" ≠ "your one run will be fine"**: you only live once and only retire once. If you happen to **draw the sample where a big crash lands in your first few years of retirement**, all-equity plus rigid withdrawals will be painful, and may force you into panic-selling at the low — **and the moment you do that, you've converted temporary volatility into permanent loss with your own hands, and lost worse than someone with a buffer.**

    So the pragmatic conclusion:

    1. **If your discipline is exceptional, your withdrawals are flexible, you hold a cash buffer, and downturns don't rattle you**: high-equity for life (VT) is supported by the data, and it's fine.
    2. **If you would panic during a retirement-phase downturn**: adding some bonds as a cushion so that you can sleep, stay invested, and avoid selling cheaply is **the better choice for you**.

    **A second-best plan you stick to always beats an optimal plan you break halfway.** In retirement that's truer than anywhere else.

## 5. Retirement isn't "set and forget"

!!! warning "You still have to check in after you retire"
    In the accumulation phase you can buy and never touch it; in the **withdrawal phase you can't fully let go**. You need to **review each year**:

    - What have markets done this year? Should you **flex your spending down**?
    - Has spending drifted upward without you noticing? (Lifestyle creep doesn't only happen before retirement.)
    - Is the buffer still worth 2–3 years? If you've spent some, should you refill it in a good year?

    Retirement planning is "**set it, protect it, check it**", not set and forget — because the rest of your life depends on it.

## 6. From accumulating to withdrawing: a mindset comparison

| | 🟢 Accumulation | 🔵 Withdrawal |
|---|---|---|
| **What matters most** | The average return | **The order of returns** (sequence risk) |
| **A downturn is** | A friend (cheap shares) | An enemy (forced to sell low) |
| **Role of bonds** | Usually unnecessary | **Downturn buffer, safety cushion** |
| **What you do** | Buy and don't touch | Review regularly, withdraw flexibly |
| **The principle** | Fixed schedule, hold long term | Keep a buffer, stay flexible, never sell cheap |

---

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    1. Picture a 40% fall in your first year of retirement while you're selling to live on — could you take it **psychologically**? That answer decides how large a bond buffer you need.
    2. Do you have 2–3 years of cash or short-dated bonds set aside? If not, when will you start building it?
    3. In a down year, which of your expenses could flex downward? List them now.

## Summary

- Accumulation is about the **average return**; retirement is about the **order of returns** — that's **sequence risk**.
- A downturn in the first few years of retirement is the most lethal: **falling prices × withdrawals** hit together and the capital never recovers.
- A downturn is a friend while accumulating (cheap shares) and an enemy while withdrawing (forced to sell low).
- Seven responses: **cash/bond buffer, flexible withdrawals, delay retirement if necessary, a lower withdrawal rate, (if you want bonds) build them over the last ten years, remember the 4% research assumed 1:1 stocks and bonds, keep fees down.**
- "VT for life" and "add bonds in retirement" don't contradict: **all-equity if you can hold on, a buffer if you'd panic** — the plan you stick to is the good plan.
- Retirement is **set / protect / check**: review it every year rather than letting go entirely.
