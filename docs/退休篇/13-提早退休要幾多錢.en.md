# Chapter 13. Early retirement & how much you need — the 4% rule and FIRE

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 3–5 · retirement planning (for anyone starting to think about stopping)**<span class="lvtag">L3–L5</span>
    Once you've been accumulating for a while, you naturally start asking: **how much do I actually need before I never have to work for money again?** This chapter gives you a very simple formula for your financial independence number, and unpacks what FIRE and "Chubby FIRE" really mean.

!!! tip "The short version"
    **Your target = annual spending ÷ withdrawal rate.** Using the 4% rule, that's **annual spending × 25**. Want more margin? Use 3.5% or 3% (×28.5 or ×33). But the hardest part isn't the arithmetic — it's **being honest about what you actually spend in a year, and about what counts as enough**.

---

## 1. Why think about early retirement at all?

"Retirement" doesn't have to mean 65, and it doesn't have to mean doing nothing. On this site we mean something broader:

!!! note "Financial independence"
    **When the returns your assets generate cover your living expenses, you are financially independent.** At that point, working becomes **your choice** rather than a requirement.

    - You can keep doing work you enjoy (without having to please anyone).
    - You can switch to something meaningful that pays less.
    - You can stop entirely — travel, raise children, pursue an interest.

**The point isn't "not working", it's getting your choices back.** That's where long-term investing is ultimately trying to take you.

---

## 2. The core formula: your financial independence number

How much is enough? The formula is simple enough to work out right now:

!!! note "Your financial independence number"
    **Target = annual spending ÷ withdrawal rate**

    Using the most popular **4% withdrawal rate**:
    **Target = annual spending × 25**

    | Withdrawal rate | Multiple | What it means |
    |---|---|---|
    | 4% | × 25 | The standard |
    | 3.5% | × 28.5 | Safer (more cushion) |
    | 3% | × 33 | Very conservative (long life or early exit) |

!!! example "A worked example (mapped to the Level framework)"
    Suppose Alex spends **$48,000** a year ($4,000 a month):

    - 4% rule: $48,000 × 25 = **$1.2M** → just past the **Level 4** threshold.
    - 3.5%: about **$1.37M** (same life, more safety margin).
    - 3%: **$1.6M** (most conservative).

    If Alex wants a bit more room and spends **$84,000** a year ($7,000 a month), the 4% rule puts the target at **$2.1M**.

    **See it? "How much you need" is entirely determined by your annual spending — spend less and the bar drops a very long way.**

## 3. Where does the 4% rule come from? Why 4%?

That "× 25" isn't arbitrary; there's evidence behind it:

!!! note "The origin of the 4% rule (Bengen / Trinity)"
    - The researcher **Bengen**, and later the **Trinity Study**, backtested US historical data: if you retire holding a portfolio of roughly **half stocks and half bonds** and withdraw **4% of your initial assets** each year (adjusted for inflation afterwards), then **across a 30-year retirement the money lasted in the large majority of cases (around 95%)**.
    - The intuition behind it: **long-run investment return minus inflation ≈ 4%**, so taking 4% a year roughly replaces itself, and in theory you never run out.

    That's why 4% is also called the **safe withdrawal rate (SWR)**.

!!! warning "But it's an idealised case, not a guarantee"
    A 95% success rate means **roughly 5% failed** — mostly because of something called **sequence risk** (a severe bear market in the first few years of retirement). It's the biggest trap in retirement planning, and the next chapter is devoted to it.

    So: **4% is a useful starting point, not an iron promise.** If you want more margin, use 3.5%.

## 4. Three flavours of FIRE: Lean / Chubby / Fat

The early retirement community (FIRE = Financial Independence, Retire Early) generally splits into three routes, differing in **the standard of living you want**:

=== "🥗 Lean FIRE"

    - **The essence**: push spending very low and get to "not having to work" as fast as possible.
    - **The life**: minimal, few luxuries, little buffer.
    - **The cost**: very little room for error; one unexpected event (medical, family) can break it.
    - **Suits**: people who value time freedom above all and don't mind frugality.

=== "🍗 Chubby FIRE (the comfortable middle) ✅"

    - **The essence**: **comfortable, with a genuine buffer** — neither extreme frugality nor luxury.
    - **The life**: travel without paying for it afterwards, live where you like, have hobbies, and survive a surprise without the plan collapsing — but not unlimited extravagance.
    - **The cost**: a stronger engine (higher income, longer time, or more discipline).
    - **Suits**: most people who want to retire early *and* live normally comfortably.

=== "🥩 Fat FIRE"

    - **The essence**: maximum comfort, essentially no spending constraint.
    - **The life**: luxurious and complicated (more tax and asset management to handle).
    - **The cost**: a **very** large number and a powerful income engine (executives, doctors, business owners).
    - **Suits**: people whose income is already high and who want to keep spending at that level in retirement.

!!! tip "The sweet spot for most people: Chubby FIRE"
    Chubby FIRE is the Goldilocks version — **considerably more comfortable than Lean, and far more achievable than Fat**. The figures thrown around online put it in a comfortable upper-middle range, but **don't let a number from the internet own you** — the real answer is always "**your own annual spending ÷ the withdrawal rate you're willing to use**".

    (The "comfortable but disciplined" framing comes from the FIRE community's Chubby FIRE concept; this is a reinterpretation of it.)

## 5. The hard part isn't the maths, it's filling in honest numbers

The formula is simple, but to get an accurate answer you have to be honest about several inputs that are **very easy to fool yourself on**:

!!! warning "The four most commonly underestimated expenses"
    1. **Tax**: dividend withholding, capital gains, how withdrawals are treated — this varies enormously between countries.
    2. **Healthcare**: costs rise with age; whether you have insurance and how much cover is a large variable.
    3. **Housing**: own or rent? In which city? This is often the single largest expense.
    4. **Buffer**: home repairs, emergencies, travel plans changing — always leave slack.

**Chubby FIRE is hard not because the maths is complicated, but because the inputs have to be honest.** Put in an optimistic "annual spending" figure and the number it produces will mislead you for the rest of your life.

---

## 6. The biggest enemy: lifestyle creep

!!! danger "'I deserve better' — this sentence will sink your plan"
    As income rises, most people unconsciously **ratchet up their standard of living**: a bigger home, a more expensive car, more lavish holidays. That's **lifestyle creep**.

    The problem is that **as your annual spending rises, your financial independence number moves further away** — turning it into a **target you can never catch**.

    > If you only do one thing for your own financial independence, make it this: **define "enough" as early as you can.** That's the difference between reaching Chubby FIRE and chasing forever.

---

## 7. How do you get there? Three engines

Once you have the target, how do you save it? There's no secret — you run three engines at once:

!!! success "The three engines of financial independence"
    1. **Raise income**: raises, changing jobs, a business, a side hustle — income is the strongest savings engine there is.
    2. **Spend deliberately**: separate needs from wants and cut what has no meaning to you (but **this is not about sacrificing quality of life or your health** — FIRE isn't asceticism).
    3. **Invest boringly**: low cost, passive index (VT), fixed schedule, held long term — everything you learned in the beginner and intermediate sections.

    **The investing part should be boring.** Exciting things (trading, thematic bets) only slow you down. Save the excitement for what you do after you retire.

---

## 8. Mapping this back to your Level

!!! note "Retirement planning × the Level framework"
    - **Levels 1–2**: focus on **starting the engines** (raising income, building saving and investing habits). The retirement number is still distant; there's no need to compute it precisely.
    - **Level 3** (investable net worth $100k–$1M): start **taking the number seriously**, stress-testing it and thinking about withdrawal rates.
    - **Levels 4–5**: most people's retirement number lands here (depending on annual spending). The focus shifts to **withdrawal strategy, tax and sequence risk** (next chapter).

    For the full ladder see [📍 Find your Level](../level.md).

    1. What do you **really** spend in a year? (Not a guess — actually add it up.) What's that × 25? Which Level does it land in?
    2. Is your "enough" a fixed number, or a target that keeps moving away?
    3. Of your three engines (income, spending, investing), which is weakest right now? What's the next step to improve it?

## Summary

- Financial independence = **asset returns cover your living expenses**; the point is getting your choices back, not "not working".
- The core formula: **target = annual spending ÷ withdrawal rate** (4% = ×25; safer options are 3.5% = ×28.5 and 3% = ×33).
- **The 4% rule** comes from the Bengen and Trinity studies (half stocks and bonds, 30 years, around 95% success), and it's **a starting point, not a guarantee**.
- Three FIRE routes: Lean / **Chubby (the comfortable middle, most people's sweet spot)** / Fat.
- The hard part is **honest inputs** (tax, healthcare, housing, buffer); the biggest enemy is **lifestyle creep** → **define "enough" early**.
- Three engines: **raise income, spend deliberately, invest boringly**.
