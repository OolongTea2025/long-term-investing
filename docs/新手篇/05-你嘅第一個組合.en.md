# Chapter 5. Your first portfolio

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 2–3 (starting out to middle) · essential for beginners**<span class="lvtag">L2–L3</span>
    Enough theory — time to actually do it. This chapter covers building your first portfolio, investing on a fixed schedule, and what rebalancing really is.

!!! tip "The short version"
    The simplest first portfolio is **a single VT** (or VTI + VXUS), bought on a fixed schedule and then left alone. If you're young, there's no rush to add bonds. Manage "choose well, buy in, sit still" and you're already ahead of most people.

---

## The simplest opening move

You don't need to overthink this. For a first portfolio, pick one of two roads:

=== "The lazy version (recommended)"

    **One VT. Done.**

    - The whole world in one holding; no need to decide a US/international split.
    - Buy on a fixed schedule, hold for the long run.
    - Suits: anyone who doesn't want the hassle and wants maximum simplicity. (Which is most people.)

=== "The hands-on version"

    **VTI + VXUS, with a split you choose.**

    - For instance US : international = 55 : 45, tilted to your own view.
    - The benefit is flexibility; the cost is that you have to rebalance yourself (see below).
    - Suits: people who want more control.

!!! tip "Fitting it to the age path"
    Remember the age path from the [Level page](../level.md)?
    **Ages 0–20** can lean toward **VOO** (US large-cap); **20–30** shifts gradually to **VT**; **from 30 onward** it's **VT** for life. The "first portfolio" above is that path made concrete.

## Investing on a fixed schedule: no need to guess the timing

The question every beginner wants to ask is: "is now a good time to get in?"

The answer: **don't guess. Buy on a schedule.**

**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** = putting in **a fixed amount** every month (or every period), regardless of whether the market is up or down.

Why do it this way?

- You can't predict short-term market moves — **nobody can** (the theory section proves this later).
- Buying a fixed amount means you automatically buy fewer shares when prices are high and more when they're low, which **averages out your cost**.
- Most importantly: **it builds discipline** and takes the decision away from your emotions. Market down? Buy. Market up? Buy.

!!! warning "A falling market is your friend"
    The most common beginner mistake is to **stop buying, or start selling, when the market falls**. But for someone still accumulating assets, a falling market = **a discount** = more shares for the same money. The people who keep buying through downturns are usually the ones laughing at the end.

## Rebalancing: what is it, and why bother?

If you chose the hands-on version (more than one holding), the proportions will drift over time.

> Example: you started at US : international = 55 : 45. After a strong run in US stocks it might be 65 : 35 — your portfolio has quietly become more concentrated and more aggressive.

**Rebalancing** = periodically (say once a year) **moving the proportions back to the targets you originally set**.

Why does it help?

- It automatically produces **"sell high, buy low"**: trim the part that ran up, top up the part that fell — exactly the correct move, and exactly the one most people can't make.
- It **controls risk**, stopping your portfolio from drifting steadily into something more dangerous than you signed up for.

!!! tip "The lazy version doesn't need this"
    If you're in the "one VT" camp, **you don't really need to rebalance at all** — VT already adjusts its global weights by market cap internally. That's another advantage of one fund: it doesn't get simpler than this.

## Should you add bonds?

If you're young and still in the accumulation phase: **usually there's no rush.**

- You have a long runway and can absorb volatility; all-equity has higher long-run returns (the study cited on the [Level page](../level.md) covers this).
- Bonds are something to consider seriously when you're **approaching retirement** and want to reduce volatility.

If you genuinely want a small comfort cushion, you can add a modest slice of a government bond ETF (a Treasury tracker, say), but it is **not a beginner requirement**. The Retirement and Intermediate sections go into this properly.

## Two practical reminders before you start

This site won't tell you which stock is about to take off, but there are two things you must keep in mind when actually executing:

1. **Keep costs low**: choose low-fee ETFs, and watch trading and currency-conversion costs. These are **pure leakage** — save what you can.
2. **Look up account and tax matters yourself**: see below.

## The practical side: this site won't teach it, you need to look it up

This site teaches **principles**, because principles are still valid in ten years. But the practical side — which account to open, what forms to file, how much tax you pay — **changes every year**. Any specific number or step anyone writes down will be out of date in a couple of years, and out-of-date information is more dangerous than none.

So this section gives no answers. It only tells you **which things you must check for yourself before you commit money**:

**① Where to open the account**
A broker in your own country, or an international one? They differ in fees, funding methods, which products you can buy, and what protections apply.

**② Tax forms**
If you buy US-domiciled ETFs (like VT) and you are not a US person, your broker will normally ask you to file a declaration that you are not a US tax resident — usually the form called **W-8BEN**. Whether you file it changes how dividend withholding is handled.

**③ Dividend withholding tax**
US-domiciled funds paying dividends to non-US persons normally have a portion withheld at source. **The treatment differs by the fund's country of domicile** — which is one of the reasons many non-US investors choose Ireland-domiciled versions (such as VWRA).

**④ US estate tax**
This is the one most people have never heard of, and the most important: **a non-US person holding "US situs assets" — which includes US-domiciled stocks and ETFs — may be exposed to US estate tax on death**, with an exemption threshold vastly lower than the one US citizens get.

  Holding Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs (such as VWRA) generally avoids the same issue — which is one of the main reasons many non-US investors use them.

  **Thresholds, rates and filing procedures all change**, so this site quotes no numbers. You only need to know **that this exists**, and to get clear on it before your portfolio grows large.

**⑤ Tax-advantaged accounts where you live**
Most countries have some form of tax-advantaged retirement or investment account. Using one can be worth more than years of clever fund selection. What they're called, who qualifies, what you can hold in them and what the limits are differ completely from country to country — so find out what exists where you live.

!!! warning "How to look these up"
    - **Go to primary sources**: your national tax authority, the official documents from your broker, and the fund issuer's own factsheets and tax notes (Vanguard, iShares, and so on).
    - **Search terms**: `W-8BEN`, `US estate tax non-resident alien`, `Irish domiciled ETF withholding tax`, `UCITS ETF` plus the name of your country.
    - **If the amount is meaningful, get a professional**: once your US-situs assets reach a level that feels significant to you, talk to a **licensed accountant or tax adviser** rather than a forum.
    - This site **is not, and cannot be**, tax advice. These things vary by person and they change.

!!! tip "But don't use this as an excuse"
    All of the above is **execution detail**, not a precondition for starting.

    The overwhelming majority of people get stuck at this step, end up doing nothing, and discover ten years later that the expensive part was never the tax — **it was the ten years they spent out of the market**.

    You already understand the concepts. The rest is an afternoon of reading.

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    1. Will you go with "one VT" or "VTI + VXUS"? Why?
    2. How much can you put in consistently each month? (A small amount is fine — **consistency** is what matters.)
    3. Decide in advance: next time the market falls, will you **keep buying** or stop? Make yourself the promise now.

## Summary

- First portfolio: **one VT** (recommended), or **VTI + VXUS** (hands-on).
- **Fixed-schedule investing**: no timing, automatic cost averaging, builds discipline; **keep buying through downturns**.
- **Rebalancing**: periodically restore your target split = automatic "sell high, buy low" + risk control (the "one VT" camp barely needs it).
- If you're young, **there's no rush to add bonds**; leave that for near retirement.
- Keep costs low.
- **VT is an example**, not the only answer — owning the whole world, cheaply, for a long time is the point. Ireland-domiciled global ETFs like VWRA do exactly the same job.
- **Accounts, W-8BEN, dividend withholding, US estate tax, local tax-advantaged accounts: look up current information yourself** (these change yearly, so this site quotes no numbers). But don't use it as an excuse — **starting is what matters most.**
