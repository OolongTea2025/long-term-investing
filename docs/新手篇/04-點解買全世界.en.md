# Chapter 4. Why buy "the whole world"?

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–2 (getting started) · essential for beginners**<span class="lvtag">L1–L2</span>
    You know you need to diversify. This chapter answers the practical question: what do you actually buy? Why "the whole world" rather than just US stocks or whatever's hot?

!!! tip "The short version"
    Buying the **global market** (VT, or VTI + VXUS) = owning several thousand companies worldwide in one purchase, with stock-specific risk near zero and no all-in bet on a single country. Don't put everything into US stocks or chase QQQ and themed ETFs — **a great company is not the same thing as a great stock**, and over-concentration will eventually get bitten by a single market's or a single sector's bad decade.

---

## VT: the whole world in one purchase

If you only want to remember one ticker, remember **VT (Vanguard Total World Stock ETF)**.

- One fund covering **US + international developed + emerging market** equities.
- Roughly **60% US, 40% outside the US** (it adjusts itself as market caps move).
- The appeal: **one stop, extremely diversified, and you never have to think about the ratio.**

For most people, VT alone, bought on a regular schedule, is genuinely enough for the long run.

!!! info "VT is an example, not the only answer"
    This site keeps using **VT** to explain things because it's simple and representative — **one fund, the whole world** — which makes it the most convenient thing to teach with.

    But what actually matters is **the principle: own the whole world, keep costs low, hold for the long run.** Not a particular ticker.

    There is more than one global-market ETF doing the same job. Investors outside the United States, for instance, often use **Ireland-domiciled versions** (UCITS ETFs such as **VWRA**), because a different domicile means different tax treatment and different ways to buy them. Other issuers offer comparable products too.

    **Which one suits you depends on where you live, which broker you use, and your own tax situation** — and those things change over time. **Don't just copy a ticker. Look up current information yourself before you commit.** The next chapter covers how to do that.

## Want to set the ratio yourself? VTI + VXUS

If you want more flexibility, two funds can produce a similar result:

- **VTI** = the whole US market
- **VXUS** = international markets outside the US

You choose the split (say US : international = 55 : 45) and can tilt it to your own view. **The benefit is flexibility; the cost is that you have to rebalance yourself.** Want simple? VT. Want control? VTI + VXUS. Both are fine.

!!! example "What's the difference between VOO and VTI?"
    - **VOO** = the S&P 500, concentrated in US **large caps** (the 500 biggest companies).
    - **VTI** = the whole **US market**: large caps plus mid and small caps.
    - Because it's market-cap weighted, VTI is still mostly large caps anyway (about 80% overlap with VOO).
    - Which is better? It depends on the era: sometimes large caps win, sometimes small caps add a bit more. Over the long run both are sound choices — **this is not worth agonising over.**

## Why not just buy US stocks? — geographic diversification

US stocks have done extremely well for many years, so people often ask: "why not just go all-in on the US?"

Two things to be careful about here:

### 1. Mean reversion: the leadership rotates
Markets have a property called **mean reversion** — periods of unusually strong performance tend to be followed by weaker ones, and vice versa.

The US will not outperform forever. There have been long stretches of history — whole decades — in which **markets outside the US beat it**. Diversifying globally means **you don't have to bet on which region wins**; you own both.

### 2. A historical lesson: don't assume one market always works
!!! warning "The Japan story"
    In the 1980s the Japanese stock market was the most impressive in the world, and everyone assumed it would keep climbing. The bubble burst in the 1990s, and the Nikkei **didn't get back to its end-1989 peak until February 2024 — thirty-four years later**.

    An investor who had gone all-in on Japan back then could have had their entire life plan destroyed. Someone globally diversified would have had the blow diluted by every other region.

    **No country is a guaranteed winner.** Global diversification means refusing to stake your life on the fortunes of one nation.

## Why not chase QQQ or hot themes?

QQQ (the Nasdaq 100) is concentrated in big tech, and its past performance has genuinely been striking. So plenty of people want to go all-in on QQQ, or buy themed ETFs for AI, EVs, cloud, space and so on.

There's a big trap here:

### Over-concentration = sector risk
A very large share of QQQ sits in technology. If tech goes through a long correction or falls out of favour, you take a heavy hit — because there are no other sectors there to cushion you.

### A great company is not a great stock
!!! warning "The most counterintuitive lesson here"
    Everyone assumes buying the biggest, most dominant companies is the safe move. But the historical data shows: **the companies with the largest market caps at any given moment have tended to underperform the market over the following decade, sometimes with outright negative returns.**

    Why? Because once a company is famous and universally admired, its share price has **already priced in** all that optimism — it's expensive, and there's nothing left on the bone. The unglamorous ones are where the room for surprise is.

    So "great company" (strong business) doesn't mean "great stock" (high return from here). Chasing whatever is currently celebrated very often means buying at the most expensive moment.

A long list of once-hot themes — from the dot-com stocks of their day through every subsequent craze — has followed the same script: mania, then the room empties. **Themed bets very rarely stay glorious for more than a decade.**

Rather than guessing which theme is the next winner, buy the whole market: **the next hot sector and the next dominant company are already inside VT.** If it rises, you're along for the ride; if it collapses, you don't go down with it.

## When you choose, come back to two criteria

Whether you pick VT or VTI + VXUS, remember what the last chapter said:

- **Low fees** (the cheaper the better; compounded over decades the difference is large).
- **Small tracking error** (it should genuinely hug the market).

The large total-market ETFs mentioned above all do well on both. **Don't chase perfection — picking a sound option and actually starting matters far more.**

    3. Does "the whole world in one purchase" feel boring to you, or reassuring?

## Summary

- **VT = the whole world in one purchase**; use **VTI + VXUS** if you want to control the ratio yourself.
- **VOO vs VTI** barely matters — both are sound, don't agonise over it.
- Don't go all-in on one country: **mean reversion** + **the Japan lesson**; no country is a guaranteed winner.
- Don't chase QQQ or themes: **over-concentration** + **"great company ≠ great stock"**.
- The criteria stay the same: **low fees, small tracking error** — then begin.
