# Chapter 1. Active or passive — which are you?

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–2 (getting started) · essential for beginners**<span class="lvtag">L1–L2</span>
    No background needed. If you can follow high-school maths, you can follow this.

!!! tip "The short version"
    If you've underperformed the market (say, VOO) three years running, then stock picking and short-term trading aren't for you. Buy an index, hold it for the long run, and you'll very likely make more money and get a lot of your time back. This chapter shows you how to **measure yourself honestly** and find out which kind of investor you are.

---

## Before you buy anything, ask yourself one question

Most people open a brokerage account and immediately start buying — someone says a stock is good, so they buy it; it goes up, they chase; it goes down, they panic. But before you put money in, it's worth stopping and asking three questions:

- Why am I getting into the market at all? Is it purely for the money?
- How much do I need before it counts as "enough"?
- Once I have it, what do I actually want it for? (A home? Children? Retiring early?)

These sound abstract, but your answers decide **which approach you should be using**. Because in the end, there are really only two roads:

| | Active investing | Passive investing |
|---|---|---|
| **What you do** | Pick stocks, time the market, trade often | Buy a basket of the whole market, hold long term |
| **Goal** | Beat the market | Capture the market's average return |
| **Time it takes** | A lot (research, watching prices) | Almost none |
| **What decides the outcome** | Whether you genuinely have an edge | Whether you can sit still |

Most people assume active is the smarter, more impressive choice. The data tells a different story. This chapter won't ask you to take my word for it — it gives you a method to **check for yourself which kind you are**.

---

## Are you actually someone who can beat the market?

There's a term for this: **excess return, or alpha**. It's simpler than it sounds:

<div class="formula" markdown>Excess return = your return − the benchmark index's return</div>

"Benchmark index" just means the broad market — say the S&P 500 (the ETF for it is **VOO**), or global equity (**VT**). That's your opponent: someone who does nothing at all except buy the index and hold it.

- If your excess return is **positive** → congratulations, you really did beat the market, and active investing makes sense for you.
- If it's **negative** → you lost to the market, and you'd be better off just buying the index and saving the time.

It sounds brutal, but it's the mirror that tells the truth. And the fact is: **the overwhelming majority of people — including a lot of professional fund managers — underperform the market over the long run.**

### An example

!!! example "Put numbers on it"
    Over the last three years, VOO returned 12% a year, and your own stock picking returned 8%.

    Excess return = 8% − 12% = **−4%**

    Which means that after three years of research and trading, you **lost by 4% to someone who did nothing but buy VOO and leave it alone**.

In that situation you don't need to calculate any further — switching to a passive index fund is already a win.

---

## Your "hourly rate" on investing

Even if you did beat the market by a little, there's a more practical question: **was it worth it?**

How many hours did you spend on financial statements, news, charts, rumours? That time has value — spent on learning a skill, extra work, or just being with your family, it pays too. So we can work out an hourly rate:

<div class="formula" markdown>Hourly rate = excess return on your total assets ($) ÷ hours spent researching</div>

Say you spent 500 hours over a year and ended up a few thousand dollars ahead of VOO. Your effective hourly rate may well be below what a part-time job pays — and that's in the case where you **won**. As for the people who **underperformed and had no excess return at all**? They spent hundreds of hours **paying for a worse outcome**.

!!! warning "The point"
    Don't just count "how much did I make". Count "how much more — or less — did I make **than doing nothing and buying the index**". That difference is the only thing that measures your actual skill.

---

## Why three years specifically?

Because **three years will almost always include both a bull market and a bear market**.

- Making money in a bull market proves nothing — everyone makes money; a monkey throwing darts makes money.
- Holding through a bear market without panic-selling and without letting fear drive you is what shows whether there's anything real there.

A good six months or a good year is very often just **luck**. Three years is long enough for the market to properly test you, and to show whether you can produce excess return across **different market conditions**.

**2022–2024** made this very clear: the market swung hard. Plenty of people who looked like geniuses in the 2020–2021 bull run, and happily called themselves one, were exposed the moment the market turned — their method had simply never been tested in a downturn. Their returns gave it all back, and some were wiped out entirely.

That experience looks cruel, but it's genuinely valuable — it forces you to find out **whether you can actually do this**, instead of feeling good about yourself on the back of one or two lucky years.

---

## Be honest with yourself: if you're behind, go passive

If your annualised return over three years is below the broad market (VOO or VT), then the answer is already clear:

> **Passive index investing suits you better.**

This isn't admitting defeat. It's **being rational, and being smart about it**. Recognising that you don't have the time or the informational edge to beat the market year after year, and then switching to a method that fits you better and costs you far less time — that's a mature decision.

And passive investing isn't "second best". For the overwhelming majority of people, **it is the best available option**: low cost, low time, and it captures the market's long-run climb.

Put the other way round: active investing only makes sense for you if you can produce excess return **consistently and repeatably** — and very, very few people can.

---

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    Get a piece of paper (or open a note) and answer honestly:

    1. Over the last three years, roughly what was my own annualised return?
    2. Over the same period, what did VOO or VT return? (You can look it up.)
    3. Subtract one from the other — is my **excess return** positive or negative?
    4. If I'd spent the research hours on learning something, extra work, or my family, would that have been worth more?

    If question 3 came out negative, don't be discouraged — you're in the same position as **almost everyone**. The good news: the chapters that follow will show you how to capture the market's average return with an index, without breaking a sweat.

---

## Summary

- **Excess return (alpha)** = your return − the market's return; only a consistently positive number counts as real skill.
- Measure over **three years**, because you need both a bull and a bear market — one or two years is very often just luck.
- Also work out your **hourly rate**: was the time you spent worth it?
- If you're behind, switch to passive. That's smart, not defeatist — for most people, passive *is* the best option.
