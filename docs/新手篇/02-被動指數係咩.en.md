# Chapter 2. What is passive index investing, and why do retail investors keep losing?

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–2 (getting started) · essential for beginners**<span class="lvtag">L1–L2</span>
    Last chapter you worked out whether active investing suits you. This one unpacks what "passive index investing" actually means, and covers one uncomfortable fact.

!!! tip "The short version"
    Passive index investing = buying an ETF that holds **the whole market**, then holding it for the long run without fiddling. Retail investors mostly lose **not because they picked the wrong stock, but because they can't sit still** — selling in fear, chasing in greed, and missing the market's handful of best days, which guts the return.

---

## "Passive index investing", one word at a time

It sounds technical. Taken apart, it isn't:

- **Index**: a thermometer that reflects how a basket of stocks is doing overall. The S&P 500 index, for example, represents the average performance of the 500 largest US companies.
- **ETF**: a fund you can buy on the stock exchange exactly like a share. Buy one, and you **own a slice of every company in the index at once** — no picking required.
- **Passive**: no frequent trading, no market timing, no guessing what will go up. You just track the index and own whatever the market contains.
- **Hold long term**: once you buy, you hold, measured in **years** — not checking and trading daily.

Put together: **buy the entire market at the lowest possible cost in money and time, then hold it patiently and take the market's long-run average return.**

## A few ETFs you'll keep hearing about

These names come up throughout the site, so it's worth getting familiar with them (no need to memorise anything):

| Ticker | What it tracks | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| **VOO** | S&P 500 (the 500 largest US companies) | US large-cap |
| **VTI** | The whole US market | Large, mid and small US stocks in one |
| **VXUS** | International markets outside the US | Everywhere except the US |
| **VT** | Global equity | **The whole world in one purchase** |
| **QQQ** | Nasdaq 100 | Concentrated in big tech (higher risk — more later) |

!!! tip "You don't need to memorise these"
    There's really only one thing to hold on to: **VT = the whole world.** For most people, long-term investing revolves around VT (or the combination of VTI + VXUS). For the rest, understanding the idea is enough.

## What is "tracking error", and why do you want it small?

A good index ETF should **stay glued** to the index it tracks — if the index rises 10%, the ETF should rise roughly 10% too.

In practice, management fees, trading costs, and small differences in holdings or weightings leave a gap between the ETF's return and the index's. That gap is called **tracking error**.

- The **smaller** the tracking error, the more faithfully the ETF reproduces the market → better.
- Large tracking error means that while you think you're buying "the market", you're really buying "the market plus a bunch of unknowns".

So there are two simple criteria for picking an ETF: **low fees, and small tracking error.** Big, long-established, large-scale total-market ETFs (like the ones above) generally do both very well.

## Here's the important part: why do retail investors keep losing?

Most people assume retail investors lose because they picked the wrong stock. They don't. **The biggest killer is not being able to sit still.**

Short-term price swings are very good at stirring up emotion:

- It drops, you get scared, you sell → you lock in the loss at the bottom.
- It rises, you get greedy or afraid of missing out, and you buy in high → you buy the top.

That back-and-forth of selling into falls and chasing rises makes you **miss the market's best few days**.

!!! warning "An uncomfortable number"
    A very large share of the stock market's long-run return comes from **a tiny number of enormous up-days**. Study after study has shown that if you're in and out of the market and **miss the ten or twenty best days**, your long-run return shrinks dramatically — easily enough to turn a gain into a loss.

    And here's the problem: **nobody knows in advance when those days will come.** They very often arrive at the point of maximum panic, right after you've sold.

So the winning approach is disappointingly simple:

> **Buy → hold → don't touch it.**

Sit still, and you automatically beat the large majority of people who are busy trading.

## Fees and discipline: the two things you actually control

You can't control how much the market rises, or when. But two things are entirely within your control, and over decades they matter enormously:

1. **Fees**: a 1% annual fee versus a 0.05% one, compounded over decades, is the difference between two different outcomes. **Choosing a low-cost ETF is free money.**

2. **Discipline**: buy on a fixed schedule, keep buying through downturns, don't panic-sell. This is your single biggest advantage — because most people can't do it.

The beautiful thing about passive investing is that it **doesn't require you to be clever**. It only requires you **not to sabotage yourself**.

---

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    1. Think back to the last big market drop (the kind the news calls a "crash"). Did you sell out of fear? What did the market do afterwards?
    2. If you had done nothing at all and simply held, where would you be today?
    3. What will you do the next time it falls? (Decide now, while things are calm — it beats improvising in the moment.)

---

## Summary

- Passive index investing = **buy the whole market cheaply, and hold it for the long run**.
- One thing to remember: **VT = the whole world.**
- Choosing an ETF: **low fees, small tracking error.**
- A retail investor's biggest enemy **isn't stock picking, it's their own emotions** — trading in and out means missing the big up-days.
- The two things you do control: **fees** and **discipline**.
