# Chapter 3. How diversification actually works — why not put everything in one or two stocks?

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–2 (getting started) · essential for beginners**<span class="lvtag">L1–L2</span>
    This chapter covers diversification, the single most central idea in long-term investing. Once it clicks, you'll see why "buying the whole world" is the smart lazy move.

!!! tip "The short version"
    Stocks carry two kinds of risk: **stock-specific risk** (which diversification can remove) and **market risk** (which it can't — and which is precisely where your return comes from). The more stocks you own, the smaller the stock-specific risk gets; own **the whole market** and it falls to nearly zero. And history says this: **wealth is created by a tiny handful of companies, while most stocks actually lose money** — so rather than guessing which one is the winner, just buy them all.

---

## Two kinds of risk, and the difference matters

Say you buy a stock. It can fall for two different kinds of reason:

1. **Stock-specific risk (unsystematic risk)**: something about that one company. Management blows up, a product fails, the industry gets into trouble, the company implodes.
   > Examples: a bank with a liquidity problem, a tech company that read the market wrong. These hit **that company only**.

2. **Market risk (systematic risk)**: the whole market falls together. A financial crisis, a pandemic, a major recession.
   > Examples: the 2008 financial crisis, the early days of 2020 — almost everything fell.

This distinction is **enormously important**, because one of them can be removed and the other cannot.

---

## Diversification: the magic that removes stock-specific risk

Imagine that instead of one stock, you buy 50, across different industries.

- Company A gets into trouble and falls, but B and C may be fine, or even up.
- Their individual pieces of bad luck **cancel each other out**.

The more stocks you own and the more spread out they are, the more those individual misfortunes average out and stop hurting you. Research shows:

> Going from one stock to about **50** across different industries removes roughly **60%** of the risk.

Eventually stock-specific risk almost vanishes, and what's left — the part you can't remove — is **market risk**.

!!! example "Diminishing returns: 30 already gets you most of the way"
    Diversification isn't a case of "more is always better" — it has **diminishing returns**. Going from 1 stock to 30 cuts risk dramatically; going from 30 to 100 improves things only slightly. So **around 30** already gives you most of the benefit. And buying a single **total-market ETF (like VT)** means owning several thousand companies at once — stock-specific risk pushed down to nearly nothing.

## Careful though: buying one industry isn't diversification

Some people buy ten stocks and think they're well diversified — but all ten are banks, or all ten are tech. That is **not diversified**.

Companies in the same industry move with the same industry cycle. When the economy rotates, the whole sector rises and falls together.

> Example: healthcare stocks during and after the pandemic went through completely different fates as a sector. Owning ten healthcare stocks means you're all on the same boat.

Real diversification spreads **across industries and across countries**. And the most thorough version of that is simply buying the whole market.

## The 99/01 rule: why stock picking is so hard to win at

This is the most counterintuitive lesson here, and the most important.

Look at the long-run history of the stock market and something startling emerges:

> **All of the stock market's wealth was created by a tiny minority of companies — somewhere around 1%.**
> At the same time, **more than 60% of stocks actually lose money over the long run.**

In other words, the odds of success and failure are **wildly asymmetric**. Pick a stock at random and your chance of catching one of the big winners is small; your chance of catching a laggard is far higher.

!!! warning "The dart-throwing monkey"
    This is exactly why you constantly hear about someone who bought the right stock and got rich — and never hear about the **90-plus percent** who got it wrong and quietly walked away poorer. This is **survivorship bias**: you see the one who won and not the crowd who lost.

    Unless you're a once-in-a-generation talent like Buffett who can pick that 1% repeatedly, **spreading out and buying all of them is what raises your odds.**

Put differently: **rather than agonising over which stock is the next winner, buy them all.** The winners are in there regardless. That is the power of buying the whole market.

## Diversification exists to get you to your goal

One last layer: what are you diversifying *for*?

- If your investing serves **a financial goal** (a home, children, early retirement) — then you need diversification **even more**. You can't afford "one wrong bet sinks the entire plan".
- If you're purely in it for the thrill of winning — concentrated bets are a different matter, but that isn't investing, it's entertainment.

For the overwhelming majority of people with a goal, diversification isn't "playing it safe" — it's **raising the probability that you get there**. That's the whole point.

---

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    1. Is what you own (or plan to own) concentrated in one or two stocks or a single industry, or genuinely spread out?
    2. If the stock you're most confident about blew up tomorrow and halved, would your plan survive?
    3. Does "just buy all of them" feel like a relief to you, or like giving something up? Why?

## Summary

- Two kinds of risk: **stock-specific** (removable) + **market risk** (not removable, and the source of your return).
- Diversification removes stock-specific risk; about **30 holdings** gets you most of the benefit, and a **total-market ETF** settles it in one purchase.
- Diversify **across industries and countries** — ten stocks in one sector doesn't count.
- **The 99/01 rule**: wealth is concentrated in a tiny number of companies and most stocks lose money → stock picking is very hard to win at, and spreading out is what improves your odds.
- If you have a financial goal, you need diversification **more**, not less.
