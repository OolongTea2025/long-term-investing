# Chapter 6. Common behavioural biases — how do you stop yourself from wrecking it?

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–3 (all beginners) · essential**<span class="lvtag">L1–L3</span>
    You already know what to do. But the biggest enemy in long-term investing is the person in the mirror. This chapter unpacks the traps the human brain keeps falling into, and gives a counter for each.

!!! tip "The short version"
    Most investing failures don't come from not knowing. They come from **a set of biases built into the human brain** that sabotage your plan at exactly the wrong moment. Recognise them and you can push back. And the **universal antidote** to nearly all of them is the same sentence: **decide your plan in advance, buy VT on a schedule, and then don't touch it.**

---

## Why devote a chapter to psychology?

The first five chapters told you what to do. But between knowing and doing sits a very wide gap, and it's called **emotion**.

Panic-selling in a downturn, chasing highs out of greed, refusing to let go of a losing position… none of this is stupidity. It's **a shared human reflex**. Professional investors fall for it too.

The good news: **if you can name it, you can resist it.** Let's go through them.

---

## The twelve most common traps

!!! example "How to use this list"
    Don't memorise it. Skim it once and notice which ones feel like they're describing you. Recognising where you personally are vulnerable is already half the battle.

**1. Prospect theory / your attitude to risk**
Everyone tolerates uncertainty differently. Chasing a slightly better return, people will take irrationally large risks — or, protecting what they already have, become excessively cautious.
> 💊 Counter: **decide the plan in advance** — which portfolio at which age — and follow it instead of improvising.

**2. Loss aversion**
Losing $100 hurts far more than gaining $100 feels good. So people "take the profit quickly before it disappears" and "hold the loser to death hoping to break even" — which means **small wins and enormous losses**.
> 💊 Counter: buy a total-market ETF. The long-run trend is upward, so short-term swings don't need to frighten you.

**3. Fear of missing out (FOMO)**
Someone brags about how much they made on X, you get itchy, you're afraid of being left behind, and you buy something you don't understand → straight into the top.
> 💊 Counter: buy VT on a schedule and stay in the market long term. Anything with a genuine future is already inside VT.

**4. Reference dependence**
Using your own purchase price as the yardstick for every decision ("I'll buy again when it comes back to my entry"), ignoring everything else that matters.
> 💊 Counter: buy on a schedule regardless of price, and your entry price can't hold you hostage.

**5. Wishful thinking**
Choosing to believe the direction you want. For instance, being convinced a theme "can't fail for the next decade" and going all-in.
> 💊 Counter: you've now looked at the alternatives, and chose the whole market on reasoning rather than betting a theme on a feeling.

**6. Confirmation bias**
Collecting only the information that supports your view, and automatically ignoring the evidence against it.
> 💊 Counter: go looking for the opposing case. (This site tries to put both sides in front of you rather than making you hunt for them.)

**7. Sunk cost fallacy**
You've already lost money or already put the effort in, so you can't let go, and you keep compounding the mistake.
> 💊 Counter: understand that fees, tax and currency conversion are **sunk costs you can't get back**, and don't make a worse decision just to avoid "realising" a loss.

**8. Endowment effect**
You unconsciously overvalue whatever you already own, and can't bring yourself to part with it.
> 💊 Counter: when you're withdrawing in retirement, sell to the plan regardless of price — don't get sentimental because you've held something a long time.

**9. Anchoring**
The first price you ever saw becomes an anchor, and anything below it feels cheap forever after.
> 💊 Counter: stick to your own buying plan, and don't treat the first price you saw as a reference point.

**10. Von Restorff effect (the vivid thing sticks)**
You remember the newest and most eye-catching things especially well (IPOs, hot themes) and follow the crowd into them.
> 💊 Counter: stay with the whole market, understand that themed bets don't stay hot, and don't get twitchy because something has been strong lately.

**11. Gambler's fallacy**
There's no pattern, but you insist on predicting anyway: "it's up so much, it has to come back" / "it's been down so long, it has to bounce".
> 💊 Counter: understand that short-run moves are close to random (the theory section proves this); you hold VT because of **long-run data**, not a short-run guess.

**12. Dunning–Kruger effect (the ignorant are the most confident)**
The less capable someone is, the more likely they are to overestimate themselves; people who genuinely know things tend to be humble.
> 💊 Counter: stay humble. When someone thumps their chest and tells you to go all-in and hold a particular stock with conviction, they usually just haven't been taught a lesson by the market yet. **There's no need to argue. Time will settle it.**

!!! tip "The whole thing, in three sentences"
    1. **Decide the plan in advance**: what to buy, how much, how often, and when to rebalance — worked out while you're calm, and written down.
    2. **Buy VT on a schedule, regardless of price**: no timing, no chasing, no panic-selling. Just discipline.
    3. **Hold long term, don't touch it**: short-term movement is normal noise and none of your business.

    Once you have a plan that's **written down and not revised in the moment**, you no longer have to make decisions at times of high emotion — and those are exactly the times people make the worst ones.

## Why can you hold on through this? — where the confidence comes from

You might ask: "when the market's falling, how do I know holding is right?"

Because you're buying the whole market and holding for the long run, and that isn't based on a feeling — it's based on **a large body of historical data**: over long horizons, global markets rise and the odds are in your favour. Your reason for buying and holding is the **long-run average return and the high success rate** that data produces — not "I think it'll go up".

> With that behind you, when the market falls you can answer yourself: **"I already ran the numbers. This kind of swing is within expectations."** And then follow the plan.

(If you want the reasoning behind those "odds" and the "long-run rise", the market theory in the optional deep dives covers it — but for actually executing, the three sentences above are enough.)

## Summary

- Investing failures usually aren't about knowledge — they're **emotion and built-in biases** wrecking things at the critical moment.
- All twelve common traps have a counter.
- **The universal antidote**: decide the plan in advance → buy VT on a schedule → hold long term and don't touch it.
- Your confidence comes from **historical data** (long-run rise, favourable odds), not from "I reckon".
