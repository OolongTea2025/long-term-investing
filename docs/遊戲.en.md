# 🎮 An Investing Life

<p class="tagline">Seventy decisions · twenty years · one control group who did nothing</p>

Chapter 6 listed twelve behavioural biases. The problem is that after reading them, almost everyone reacts the same way:

> "Yes, obviously, I know all that."

**Knowing and doing are two different things.** This game exists to prove that sentence.

---

## Before you play, a few things

**First, the important caveat: the game is in Cantonese only, and has not been translated.** The interface and all of its text are in written Cantonese, the language the original edition of this site is written in. It wasn't left untranslated out of laziness — the whole thing turns on tone of voice, on options that sound perfectly reasonable in the moment, and a flat translation would lose exactly the thing it is trying to demonstrate. So if you read Chinese, do play it; it's the best part of this site. If you don't, this page explains what it does and what it finds, and you lose nothing from the argument.

You play a thirty-year-old with **HK$300,000** (all amounts in the game are in Hong Kong dollars) who saves a little each quarter.

A character called **Mike** starts alongside you. He buys index funds on day one, keeps contributing every quarter, and does nothing else for twenty years — he's your control group.

Seventy rounds, one choice each. Some options are biases and some aren't, but **in the moment, every one of them feels sensible**. That's the whole point.

!!! warning "This is not telling you to stay out of the market"
    Most ways of playing the game lose to Mike. You might come away thinking the conclusion is "investing is gambling, don't touch it". **It isn't.**

    Biases aren't a sign you're stupid — they're standard equipment in the human brain, and professionals have them too. Precisely because you can't train them away, the answer isn't "be smarter", it's **to route around your brain with a system**: buy the whole market, contribute a fixed amount, then don't look.

    Mike isn't a genius. He simply **removed his own brain** from the decision loop.

## What you get when you finish

The big difference between this and most games of its kind is that it **opens up the arithmetic** for you.

**1. Where the gap actually came from (in Hong Kong dollars)**

The game takes the exact market path you just played and your exact seventy decisions, switches off one mechanism at a time, and re-runs it. The difference is what that one thing cost you:

| Mechanism | Corresponds to |
|---|---|
| Trading costs | [Chapter 2 · fees eating your return](新手篇/02-被動指數係咩.md) |
| Being out of the market (panic selling, waiting for a dip) | [Chapter 2 · missing the best days](新手篇/02-被動指數係咩.md) |
| Concentrated positions | [Chapter 3 · how diversification works](新手篇/03-分散風險原理.md) |
| Leverage | [Chapter 7 · allocation path & leverage](進階篇/07-配置路線與槓桿.md) |
| Your salary | [Chapter 7 · human capital](進階篇/07-配置路線與槓桿.md) |

Because it reuses the same path, this number is **exact**, not an estimate.

**2. Whether it was skill or luck**

If you beat Mike, the game takes the seventy decisions you actually made, drops them unchanged into **400 brand-new market histories**, and re-runs them — same behaviour, different markets.

- You win in most of those universes → genuine skill.
- You only won this once → luck.

That's the **survivorship bias** described in [Appendix A](附錄/01-邏輯謬誤與思維.md), demonstrated on your own playthrough.

**3. Every bias you triggered links back to the chapter that covers it.**
