# Chapter 8. 100% stocks for life? — the paper that challenges the conventional wisdom

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 2–4 · optional intermediate reading (only if you're interested)**<span class="lvtag">L2–L4</span>
    Conventional wisdom says the older you get, the more you should cut equities and add bonds. But a major recent academic paper, using nearly 130 years of data across 38 countries, reaches a genuinely startling conclusion. This chapter helps you understand what it says, how credible it is, and what it means for you in practice.

!!! tip "The short version"
    *Beyond the Status Quo* (Cederburg and colleagues) simulated on a huge body of historical data and concluded: **holding "50% domestic equity + 50% international equity" for life, with no bonds at all, beat the traditional "cut equities and add bonds as you age" strategy on retirement wealth, retirement spending, capital preservation and bequests alike.** But it comes with a cost you must face honestly — **larger volatility and deeper drawdowns**. This chapter isn't telling you to follow it blindly; it's helping you see the whole picture.

---

## 1. What does conventional wisdom say?

You've certainly heard these pieces of "common sense":

1. **Diversify between stocks and bonds**: don't hold only equities; mix in bonds for stability.
2. **The older you are, the fewer equities**: be aggressive when young, and shift gradually into bonds as retirement approaches to protect the capital.

That's the design behind the very popular **target-date fund (TDF)** — a **glidepath** that automatically cuts equities as you age: close to 100% equities when young, perhaps only 10–20% equities after retirement with the rest in bonds and cash. It's the default option in a great many retirement plans.

Sounds reasonable, doesn't it? But this paper argues: **it may leave you poorer in retirement.**

---

## 2. What did the paper actually do?

!!! note "Method (simplified)"
    - **Data**: real returns from **38 developed countries between 1890 and 2019**, nearly 2,500 country-months. That breadth exists to **reduce the survivorship bias of using US data alone** (the US has been a winner for the last century, which is no guarantee any country will be that lucky in future).
    - **Model**: it simulates a US couple who start work at 25, save 10% of income and retire at 65, including the ups and downs of their earnings, social security, and even when they die (longevity risk).
    - **Simulations**: **one million** Monte Carlo runs using a **block bootstrap**, to see how each strategy performs across an enormous range of possible futures.

It compared several strategies, including traditional TDFs, balanced stock-and-bond portfolios (60/40, say), 100% domestic equity, and **an all-equity portfolio of "50% domestic + 50% international"**.

---

## 3. The conclusion: globally diversified all-equity wins across the board

The paper's central finding:

!!! success "The 50-domestic / 50-international all-equity portfolio dominated almost every outcome"
    Compared with the traditional strategies, holding a globally diversified all-equity portfolio for life produced **better medians** on:

    - **Wealth accumulated by retirement** ✅ more
    - **Money available to spend in retirement (replacement rate)** ✅ higher
    - **Probability of running out of money** ✅ lower
    - **What's left as a bequest** ✅ more

    The paper even estimates that if every retirement saver in the US switched to this strategy, the potential welfare gain would run into **trillions of dollars**.

A few details that are counterintuitive but important:

- **Bonds are not free safety**: the simulations show that **adding bonds or short-term bills on top of an all-equity base raises the savings rate you need to reach the same retirement outcome** — the more bonds you mix in, the more you have to save to compensate, because bonds grow more slowly over the long run.
- **Geographic diversification > stock/bond diversification**: the key isn't mixing stocks with bonds, it's **mixing domestic equity with international equity**. The research even suggests that a split of roughly **30% domestic, 70% international** performed best in their model. This echoes the beginner section's "why buy the whole world" — don't go all-in on one country.

## 4. Does this paper agree with this site's position?

Very much so. This site's age path — **VT from 30 onward, held into old age, retirement included** — is exactly the practical form of what this paper argues:

| What the paper says | How this site corresponds |
|---|---|
| Hold globally diversified equity for life | **One VT** *is* "domestic + international" global equity, settled in one purchase |
| Don't add bonds too early or too heavily | When young, **no rush to add bonds**; leave them for near retirement |
| Regional diversification matters more than stock/bond diversification | The beginner section keeps stressing **buying the whole world (VT)**, not going all-in on the US |

Put differently: **you don't need to build "50 domestic / 50 international" yourself — buy VT and it has already diversified you by global market cap.**

## 5. But be honest about the costs and the criticisms

This site doesn't cheerlead for anything one-sidedly. The paper is contested, and you should know it:

!!! warning "Cost 1: larger volatility and deeper drawdowns"
    100% equities = **no bond cushion**. **Maximum drawdowns are more extreme in both the working years and retirement** — in a severe bear market you may watch your net worth halve. The paper acknowledges this as the principal concern; it simply argues the higher long-run return is worth it. **The question is whether you can hold on.** If you can't, and you panic-sell, everything before it was wasted.

!!! warning "Cost 2: sequence risk (most lethal early in retirement)"
    If a severe bear market hits **in the first few years after you retire**, you're selling to fund living costs while prices keep falling. This is **sequence risk**, and it's the biggest weak point of all-equity retirement. The topic is important enough that the **Retirement section** deals with it specifically.

!!! warning "Criticism 3: history isn't the future"
    - Some academics question whether the data is still tilted toward periods when equities won, and may not cover future unknowns like inflation regimes and geopolitics.
    - Others (communities such as Bogleheads and Early Retirement Now) argue that ignoring bonds magnifies risk in the retirement phase, and that it doesn't sufficiently account for individual risk tolerance or tax.
    - Some argue it **understates risk-adjusted return** and so overstates the appeal of pure equity.

    The paper has been nominated for academic awards and widely discussed in the mainstream financial press — it's "substantial but contested" research. **Good to consider, not to follow blindly.**

## 6. So what should I actually do?

!!! tip "A pragmatic stance"
    1. **Accumulation phase (young to middle-aged)**: the paper and this site agree — **all-equity (VT), held long term**, with no rush to add bonds. There isn't much dispute here.
    2. **Approaching or in retirement**: this is where the real disagreement lives. "100% equities for life" has the highest theoretical return, **but you have to be able to carry the drawdowns and the sequence risk**. If you can't, and you'd panic-sell in a downturn, then **adding some bonds as a comfort cushion so you can sleep and stay invested may be the better choice for you** — because **a second-best plan you stick to beats an optimal plan you abandon halfway**.
    3. Whichever you choose, **the key is to think it through in advance, write it down, and not change it in the moment**.

    The Retirement section uses the 4% rule and sequence risk to help you make that choice concretely.

## Summary

- *Beyond the Status Quo* simulated 38 countries over 130 years and concluded: **"domestic + international" all-equity for life beat the traditional "add bonds with age" strategy across the board**.
- The key insights: **bonds are not free safety** (they drag on long-run growth); **geographic diversification matters more than stock/bond diversification**.
- This maps exactly onto this site's position of **holding one VT into old age**.
- **The cost**: larger volatility and drawdowns, plus sequence risk early in retirement; academics also raise "history ≠ future" and "understated risk" criticisms.
- In practice: **all-equity in the accumulation phase is not very controversial**; **whether you want a bond cushion in retirement depends on whether you can hold on** — and a plan you can hold is the good plan.
