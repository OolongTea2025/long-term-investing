# Chapter 12. Other assets and myths — gold, high dividends, and your "real" return

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–5 · intermediate (the closing chapter; useful to everyone)**<span class="lvtag">L1–L5</span>
    The last chapter of the intermediate section takes apart three of the most popular investing myths: **does gold hedge inflation? can you live off dividends? is the return your app shows actually the return you pocket?** Once you've read it, these plausible-sounding claims won't fool you again.

!!! tip "The short version"
    **Gold**: it doesn't hedge inflation over short or medium horizons, it's very volatile, and unless you're holding for 40 years it doesn't belong in your allocation. **High dividends**: dividends aren't an independent factor; the returns of high-dividend strategies are really indirect exposure to the **profitability and investment factors** — if you want that return, factor investing gets it more efficiently. **Your real return (MWRR)**: it's almost certainly lower than the return a fund advertises (TWRR), and the gap comes from your own moving in and out — touch it less and the gap narrows.

---

## 1. Gold: don't hold it long term

"Buy gold in troubled times." "Gold hedges inflation." You've certainly heard both. What does the data say?

!!! warning "Myth 1: gold hedges inflation"
    Research (NBER) shows that **there is essentially no correlation between gold and unexpected inflation.** From 1975 to 2011, the two were positively correlated only briefly around 1980, and **failed to line up** the rest of the time.

    Which means: you assume gold will protect you during inflation, but **it doesn't reliably do so on schedule**. Buying gold as an inflation hedge is a bet, not a dependable hedge.

Then there's gold's **fundamental problem**:

!!! note "Why gold is so hard to value"
    - Gold has **no cash flow**: unlike stocks with earnings or bonds with interest, gold **produces nothing**.
    - Gold has **no maturity and no fixed value**: it's very hard to say what it "should" be worth.
    - **Real prices swing enormously**: the volatility is similar to nominal prices, and it mean-reverts on a cycle of roughly **a decade**.

!!! danger "The fair-value framework for gold"
    Erb and Harvey (*The Golden Dilemma*, 2013), building on Jastram's "golden constant" framework, estimate that over the long run **inflation is the main driver of the gold price** — but the swings are so large that:

    > If you buy when the **real gold price is high**, then on a fair-value basis your **expected real return over the following 10 years may be negative** (their estimate at the time was about −4% a year, or −12% a year if gold fell back to its historical lows).

    **Conclusion: unless your investment horizon is 40 years or more, we don't recommend adding gold to your allocation.** For the overwhelming majority of people — **gold isn't your thing.**

## 2. High dividends: the "passive income" you think you're getting is actually…

"Buy high-dividend stocks, sit back and collect the income, never work again" — it's a common dream. To take it apart, you first need to understand one thing:

!!! quote "The single most important sentence"
    **No asset pricing model treats "dividends" as an independent factor.**

    Meaning: within the academic framework of where returns come from, **whether a company pays a dividend simply isn't a factor that explains returns.** A stock's eventual annualised return is similar whether or not it pays dividends — because a dividend just moves value from one pocket (the share price) to another (cash in your hand), leaving the total unchanged. This is **dividend irrelevance**.

So why do "dividend growers" — the aristocrats that have raised payouts for 25 years or more — seem to beat the market with lower volatility?

!!! note "The truth: high dividends = indirect factor exposure"
    Factor analysis shows that **high-dividend and dividend-growth strategies are really indirect exposure to the profitability factor and the investment factor** — and those are **the actual reason** they outperform, not the dividend itself.

    Put differently: you think you're buying "dividends", but you're buying "**companies that earn well and don't burn money expanding**". And **factor investing captures that exposure more directly and more efficiently.**

!!! warning "The drawbacks of a high-dividend strategy"
    - **The factor exposure is unstable and inconsistent.**
    - **Insufficient diversification**: chasing yield easily leaves you **concentrated in a handful of stocks or sectors**, carrying single-stock risk.
    - **A high yield can be a trap**: a high payout ratio may mean the share price has already collapsed, or that the dividend **isn't sustainable** (a last flourish before the business deteriorates). Plenty of historical "high-yield blue chips" later slashed their dividends and halved in price.

!!! tip "What to do instead"
    - Want that return? **Do factor investing directly** (see Chapter 10), or buy a dividend-growth ETF (VIG, NOBL) — no stock picking and no single-stock risk. (⚠️ Both are US-domiciled: without a US tax treaty, 30% of the dividends is withheld — and dividends are their whole selling point, so the drag shows. See Chapter 5.)
    - **Even Buffett is not a dividend investor**: his 2012 shareholder letter spends pages explaining why Berkshire itself **pays no dividend** — shareholders who want cash do better over time by selling a few shares each year. AQR's analysis found his returns come from the **value and profitability factors**, not dividends. **Don't call him a dividend-growth investor.**

## 3. Your "real" return: TWRR vs MWRR

The last myth, and possibly the one that hits closest to home. You open your broker's app and see "this fund returned 11% a year over the last decade" — but is that 11% **actually what you pocketed**?

Very possibly **not**. Because there are two ways to calculate it:

!!! note "Two ways of measuring return"
    - **TWRR (time-weighted rate of return)**: only counts the price movement of **the investment itself**, and **ignores** when you put money in or took it out. → Used to measure **the fund or ETF's own performance** (the manager is responsible for the holdings, not for when you buy and sell). **The number in the advertisement is usually TWRR.**
    - **MWRR (money-weighted rate of return)**: includes **the timing of your own inflows and outflows**. → This is the return **you actually pocketed**.

**The key fact: MWRR is usually lower than TWRR.** Why?

!!! danger "The behaviour gap"
    Because most people's money moves at exactly the wrong times: **buying greedily into rising markets (expensive) and selling in panic on the way down (cheap)**. That buy-high, sell-low behaviour **dilutes** your actual return.

    Morningstar's *Mind the Gap* 2022 report (the ten years 2012–2021): across the same set of funds, **TWRR was around 11.0% while investors' actual MWRR was around 9.3%** — a full **1.7 percentage points** evaporating into thin air. That's the behaviour gap.

    And: **the more volatile the portfolio, the larger the gap.** Investors in low-volatility portfolios stay much closer to the true TWRR, because they're less easily frightened into action.

!!! success "How do you close the gap between MWRR and TWRR?"
    1. **Discipline: stop moving in and out.** Set the plan and don't trade until you reach the goal.
    2. **Choose a portfolio whose volatility you can bear**: too much volatility and you won't be able to keep your hands off it → the gap widens. (Which echoes Chapter 8: **a plan you can hold is the good plan.**)
    3. **Fixed-schedule investing + holding long term**: mechanical execution removes market timing, the biggest killer of returns.

    **Ultimately: half your return is what the market gives you (TWRR), and the other half is decided by your own behaviour (the gap between TWRR and MWRR). The second half is the part you actually control.**

## 4. Closing out the intermediate section

Congratulations! If you've read this far, you've finished the whole **🟡 Intermediate** section — from allocation paths, the truth about leverage and the 100%-equity argument, through what risk really is, factor investing and bonds, to gold, dividends and real returns. Condensed:

!!! quote "The spirit of the intermediate section"
    1. **Allocation > stock picking**: diversify across time, region, asset and factor; choose an allocation **you can hold**.
    2. **No leverage** (mortgage on your own home excepted); **no timing bets** (not on long bonds, not on gold).
    3. **Real risk = permanent loss**, not volatility; carry compensated risk (market, term, factors) and avoid uncompensated risk (concentration, default).
    4. **Factors and high dividends are both, fundamentally, factor exposure** — if you want it, do it directly and with discipline rather than through a myth.
    5. **The source of return you control most is your own behaviour.** Touch it less, win more.

## Summary

- **Gold**: doesn't hedge inflation over short or medium horizons, very volatile, no cash flow so hard to value; keep it out of the allocation unless you're holding 40 years.
- **High dividends**: dividends aren't an independent factor; those returns are really **profitability + investment factor** exposure, and factor investing captures it more efficiently; watch out for yield traps and concentration risk.
- **TWRR vs MWRR**: the advertisement quotes TWRR, you pocket MWRR, and the latter is usually lower — the gap comes from **buying high and selling low** (and it widens with volatility).
- To close the gap: **discipline, don't fiddle, choose a portfolio you can hold, invest on a schedule for the long run.**
- **The return you control most is your own behaviour.**
