# Chapter 10. Factor investing — only if you want to go a step further

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 1–5 · optional intermediate reading (Level 1 is plenty; don't force it)**<span class="lvtag">L1–L5</span>
    VT is already a very good answer. But if you want to understand why some funds beat the market over long periods, and to squeeze out a little more efficiency, you need to understand **factors**. This chapter goes from CAPM to the five-factor model, covers the methodology, portfolios for Levels 1–5, and a lazy shortcut called **AVGV**.

!!! danger "Before you read, remember this"
    **Factor investing is advanced, optional, and not required.** For the overwhelming majority of people, **getting to the "Level 1 allocation" is enough**, and holding only VT is completely fine. Factor investing demands that you **genuinely understand it and can tolerate tracking error**. Buy it without understanding it and you'll very likely abandon it halfway and end up worse off. **Build the total-market portfolio first, improve your income and your life, and only then look into factors if you have the time and energy.**

---

## 1. Start with CAPM: where does return come from?

Modern finance theory tries to answer one question: **what actually determines a stock's return?**

The earliest answer was the **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)**:

!!! note "CAPM in one sentence"
    Your return comes from the **market risk (beta, β)** you bear — the **systematic risk that cannot be diversified away** (in 2008, everything fell together).

    **Formula:** E(Rᵢ) = R_f + βᵢ × [ E(R_m) − R_f ]

    (Expected return of stock i = risk-free rate + its β × the market risk premium)

When you buy VT, you are carrying that **market risk** in exchange for **the market's return**. That's the theoretical foundation of passive investing.

**But — CAPM only explains about two-thirds of the variation in returns.** Researchers found that certain stocks (**small caps and value stocks**) had long-run returns **higher than CAPM said they should**, leaving an unexplained lump of excess return (alpha). Why?

---

## 2. Three factors → five factors: more sources of return

To explain those anomalies, **Fama and French (in the 1990s)** proposed the **three-factor model**: two more alongside the market factor.

| Factor | Meaning | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | Systematic market risk | You carry it just by owning stocks |
| **Size (SMB)** | Small caps vs large caps | Over the long run, small caps returned slightly more |
| **Value (HML)** | Value stocks vs growth stocks | Over the long run, unglamorous but cheap stocks returned slightly more |

The three-factor model explains around **90%** of the variation in returns. Adding **momentum** gives the **four-factor model** (around 95%), and Fama–French later added two more to make the **five-factor model**:

| Added factor | Meaning | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|
| **Profitability (RMW)** | High-profit vs low-profit companies | Companies that earn well tend to perform better long term |
| **Investment (CMA)** | Conservative vs aggressive investment | Companies that don't burn money expanding tend to do better long term |

!!! example "The five-factor model (no need to memorise; get the idea)"
    E(Rᵢ) = R_f + β₁·market + β₂·size + β₃·value + β₄·profitability + β₅·investment

    Every stock has different **loadings (β)** on each factor. Factor investing means **deliberately increasing your exposure to some of those factors** in exchange for that factor's long-run premium.

---

## 3. The key question: why does buying VT leave only the market factor?

A lot of people get stuck here. VT is the whole market, so it contains value stocks, small stocks and momentum stocks — why does the factor exposure "disappear"?

!!! note "Because the factors cancel each other out"
    - VT holds both **value stocks** and **growth stocks** → their value-factor exposures **cancel**, netting to zero.
    - It holds both **high-momentum** and **low-momentum** stocks → momentum cancels too.
    - The result: buying the whole market means the factors **offset in pairs**, leaving only the **market factor (β)**.

So if you **don't want the factors to cancel and want to deliberately keep exposure to one of them**, you have to buy **an additional ETF that tracks that specific factor** on top of the whole market (a small-cap value ETF, say). That's what factor investing actually looks like in practice.

---

## 4. Is there a reason behind the factors, or is it just data mining?

A perfectly reasonable suspicion: might these factors just be coincidences dredged out of old data, which will stop working in future?

The academic consensus is that **a genuine factor premium usually has one of two causes**:

!!! quote "The two sources of a factor premium"
    1. **Risk compensation (risk-based)**: the factor represents some **additional systematic risk**, and the market pays you more for carrying more risk. (Small caps and value stocks are especially fragile in bad economies, so you demand a premium before you'll hold them.)
    2. **Behavioural bias**: because people are **irrational** (overconfidence, over-extrapolation, loss aversion…), assets get **mispriced**, leaving a bargain for whoever spots it. (Momentum is largely supported by investor behavioural bias.)

**Why does the distinction matter?**

- The **risk-compensation** kind (market, size, value, profitability, investment) is more **durable**, because risk doesn't disappear just because everyone knows about it.
- The **mispricing** kind (momentum) has **a stronger premium but a weaker risk foundation** — once too many people pile in and money creates factor **crowding**, the premium can be squeezed dry.

!!! warning "Factors aren't guaranteed winners, and they have dead periods"
    - **They can fail out of sample**: once a factor is published, the more people trade it, the more the mispricing gets arbitraged away and returns fall.
    - **They can underperform for years on end**: in certain periods factor investing has visibly lagged the market (the value factor endured a long stretch of this).
    - **Trading costs and fees**: factor ETFs usually cost more than VT, and that eats part of the premium.

    **Remember: the long-run case for factors is still reasonable, but you have to endure the tracking error and the psychological grind of trailing VT for several years running.** That's the hard part.

## 5. Factor ETF methodology: exposure × premium − fees

Choosing a factor ETF isn't as simple as reading the name. Keep this formula in mind:

!!! note "A factor ETF's real return"
    **Real return = factor loading × factor premium − fees**

    - **The loading** is determined by the ETF's **methodology**: the stricter the screen and the more concentrated the holdings, the higher the exposure and the larger the potential premium (and the larger the volatility).
    - So when you choose a factor ETF, you must look at **how it selects stocks**, not just the label.

!!! danger "The cardinal sin of factor investing"
    **Buying a big pile of different factor ETFs that end up cancelling each other out, so you get exactly the same result as holding VT — but paid more in fees.** That's the stupidest possible ending. If you're going to do factor investing, **choose carefully and combine with a purpose**, rather than collecting a handful at random.

## 6. Level 1–5 factor portfolios (a reference framework)

Here's a reference framework that goes from shallow to deep. **Again: under normal circumstances, getting to Level 1 is enough.** The further down you go, the greater the volatility and risk — don't touch what you don't understand.

!!! example "Level 1 — where a normal index investor should stop ✅"
    75% in the market factor, 25% in the small / value / profitability factors. The core is VTI + VXUS with a modest small-value sleeve.

    | ETF | Weight | Role |
    |---|---|---|
    | VTI | 42% | Whole US market |
    | VXUS | 33% | Whole international market |
    | AVUV | 14% | US small-cap value |
    | AVDV | 11% | International small-cap value |

!!! example "Level 2 — don't buy this unless you really understand it"
    On top of small value, add the **momentum factor** to diversify further and improve risk-adjusted return (roughly 6:4 on a risk-parity logic). The number of ETFs grows (VTI/VXUS/AVUV/AVDV plus QVAL/IVAL for value and QMOM/IMOM for momentum). **Momentum has the strongest premium, but higher fees, larger swings and a weaker risk foundation.**

!!! example "Level 3 — for people who know factor investing inside out"
    Almost everything in the small / value / momentum factors (AVUV/AVDV/QVAL/IVAL/QMOM/IMOM). **Very high volatility, very high risk, chasing high returns.** This level is already unsuitable for most people.

!!! danger "Levels 4 and 5 — not recommended"
    Level 4 = the Level 3 portfolio at **2× leverage**; Level 5 = more concentrated plus 2× leverage. **This site does not recommend any form of LETF or leveraged factor investing** (see Chapter 7: liquidation and volatility decay). They're listed only for completeness, **not as a suggestion**.

!!! tip "Want lower risk?"
    An all-factor portfolio can **add high-grade (government) bonds** — for instance at a 4:6 ratio of factors to bonds — to bring the overall volatility down.

## 7. The lazy shortcut: AVGV (global factors in one fund)

If you're convinced by factor investing but **don't want to assemble a pile of ETFs yourself**, there's one worth knowing about:

!!! note "AVGV — Avantis All Equity Markets Value ETF"
    - **One ETF that already combines global (US + international + emerging) value and small-value exposure internally**, so you don't have to assemble it and don't risk buying combinations that cancel out.
    - Expense ratio around 0.26% (higher than VT, but reasonable for "global factors in one click").
    - Factor analysis: the exposure is still mostly **the market factor**, with smaller loadings on size (SMB), value (HML) and investment (CMA) — in other words it's "VT plus a layer of factor tilt".
    - Rough expectation: slightly higher long-run return than VT (historical simulations suggest something like 1–2 percentage points), **but with higher volatility too**. No free lunch.

!!! tip "How to use AVGV"
    - **Want it easy but with some factor exposure**: pair **AVGV + VT** (VT as the base, AVGV as the tilt).
    - **Strongly convinced by factors**: you could hold AVGV alone.
    - **Not sure**: just hold VT, and that's completely fine — **factors are a bonus question, not a required one.**

## 8. Some common questions

!!! question "Why not just go all-in on small-cap value?"
    Because that's **over-concentration**: the expected return may not materialise, and **the tracking error will make you deeply regret it** (several years of trailing VT, and plenty of people give up and sell at the bottom). You want exposure spread across **different factors**, because they take turns being strong and weak, and only diversification makes it sustainable.

!!! question "Is factor investing active or passive?"
    **Somewhere in between.** It's rule-based, transparent and replicable (like passive), but deliberately departs from market-cap weighting to bet on factors (like active). Academically it's called **smart beta**.

!!! question "I'm a committed value investor — what are my options?"
    At the extreme you could simply buy Buffett's **BRK.B** (AQR analysed it and found it is essentially exposure to the **value + profitability** factors). But you have to **accept single-stock risk** (will the company sustain that style after Buffett?). **A factor ETF is value investing that doesn't depend on one individual.**

## Summary

- Where return comes from: CAPM has only the **market factor**; three factors add **size and value**; five factors add **profitability and investment** (plus momentum).
- Buying VT leaves only the **market factor**, because the others **cancel out**; keeping a factor means buying an additional factor ETF.
- Factor premia come from **risk compensation** (more durable) or **behavioural bias / mispricing** (more easily arbitraged away).
- Factors are **not guaranteed winners**: they can stop working, trail for years, and cost you fees and tracking error — **whether you can endure it is the real question.**
- Factor ETF return = loading × premium − fees; **the cardinal sin is buying a random pile that cancels out**.
- **Level 1 is already enough for most people**; Levels 4 and 5 (leverage) are **not recommended**.
- The lazy shortcut: **AVGV** (global factors in one click), or **VT + AVGV**; if you're unsure, just hold VT — that's fine.
