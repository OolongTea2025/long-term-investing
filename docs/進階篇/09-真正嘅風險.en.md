# Chapter 9. What risk really is — volatility isn't risk

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 2–5 · intermediate (essential if you want to genuinely understand risk)**<span class="lvtag">L2–L5</span>
    Most people think risk means "the market swings a lot". But the investor Howard Marks argues that **understanding risk that way gets the most fundamental thing wrong.** This chapter redefines risk, then works through what your VOO / VTI / VT / 60-40 is actually exposed to.

!!! tip "The short version"
    **Real risk isn't volatility, it's the possibility of permanent loss.** You can ride out volatility (don't sell and it comes back); permanent loss doesn't come back. And the future is **a set of probabilities**, not one definite answer — which means risk can **never be quantified precisely**. Understand that and short-term swings stop freezing you.

---

## 1. How does academia define risk, and why does Howard Marks disagree?

Open a textbook and risk is usually defined as **volatility** — the standard deviation of returns. The more the market moves, the higher the risk.

Why does academia like volatility so much? Because it's **quantifiable, model-friendly, and produces a number**. Convenient.

But **Howard Marks**, founder of Oaktree Capital, pushed back hard on this in his classic memo *Risk Revisited*:

!!! quote "Marks's core point"
    What investors actually fear is **not temporary volatility, but permanent loss**.

    > "We can ride out volatility, but we never get a chance to undo a permanent loss."

Where's the difference?

- **Volatility**: VT falls 30% this year, you don't sell, and a few years later it's recovered — you **lost nothing**, you just rode a rollercoaster.
- **Permanent loss**: you **panic-sold** during that 30% fall, or you bought something whose fundamentals are permanently broken and never comes back (a company that blew up). That's real loss.

!!! note "The two causes of permanent loss"
    1. **Being forced or frightened into selling at the bottom**: emotional collapse, needing the money, or being liquidated because you used leverage. → This is exactly **why we tell you not to use leverage** (see Chapter 7).
    2. **Fundamentals deteriorate and the asset never recovers**: betting on one company and it collapses. → This is exactly **why you buy the whole market rather than individual stocks** (see the beginner section).

**People who can ride out volatility survive; permanent loss has no way back.** That sentence is the spine of this chapter.

## 2. The future is a set of probabilities, not an answer

Marks's second important point: **the future can't be predicted precisely, because it isn't a definite outcome at all — it's a probability distribution.**

!!! example "Elroy Dimson's line"
    > **"Risk means more things can happen than will happen."**

    History only ever runs **one version**, which makes us assume in hindsight that everything was inevitable — when at the time there were countless possible outcomes. That's the hindsight illusion.

A few conclusions worth keeping:

- **Knowing the probability ≠ knowing the outcome**: you know the chance of rolling a 7 is 1 in 6, but you can't predict the next roll.
- **In the end only one thing happens**: so even if a path has the highest expected value, you have to reject it if it **contains one extreme bad outcome you can't survive**. (Marks uses skydiving: even at 95% success, when the 5% is death, you don't jump.) **Investing isn't a bet on "highest expected value", it's a bet on "I'm still alive in the worst case".**

Which is why — **anyone claiming they can precisely calculate the maximum your portfolio can fall is overconfident.**

## 3. The real relationship between risk and return

There's a popular chart: higher risk → higher return, a nice straight line.

**That chart is wrong**, or at the very least badly misleading. Marks's corrected version goes like this:

!!! note "The correct risk–return relationship"
    - Higher-risk assets do have a higher **expected** return — otherwise nobody would buy them.
    - But **at the same time the range of possible outcomes widens, and the bad tail on the left gets considerably worse.**
    - Which means: it isn't "high risk guarantees high return", it's "**an asset that looks riskier has to offer a higher expected return before investors will hold it**".

    > "If riskier investments could be counted on to produce higher returns, they wouldn't be riskier."

## 4. There isn't one risk: there are 24

Marks points out that "risk" isn't a single thing at all; he lists at least **24 kinds**. Don't memorise them — skim once and get a feel for how many faces it has:

!!! example "The many faces of risk (selected and organised)"
    - **Core loss**: risk of permanent loss, risk of falling short of your goal, risk of missing out (FOMO)
    - **Credit and markets**: credit risk, liquidity risk, concentration risk, leverage risk, funding risk
    - **Valuation and price**: volatility risk, model risk, black swan risk, valuation risk, basis risk
    - **Others**: interest-rate risk, purchasing-power (inflation) risk, event risk, manager risk, career and headline risk, over-diversification ("diworsification"), upside risk (missing the rise)…

**The point isn't to memorise them** but to understand one thing: **reducing one kind of risk very often increases another.** For example, mixing in a lot of bonds to cut volatility increases your **purchasing-power risk** (inflation quietly eats you) and your **shortfall risk** (returns aren't enough to fund retirement). **There is no free lunch.**

## 5. Six counterintuitive properties of risk

This section is Marks at his most distilled, and worth pinning to the wall:

!!! quote "Counterintuitive, but true"
    1. **The most dangerous moment is precisely when everyone believes there's no risk.**
    2. **An asset gets safer as it falls and riskier as it rises** (the cheaper the valuation, the higher the future expected return, and vice versa).
    3. **Risk only reveals itself in bad conditions** — you find out who was swimming naked when the tide goes out.
    4. Risk has many dimensions, and they **conflict with each other** (reducing one usually raises another).
    5. **Risk management can't be outsourced** — the person making the decision has to carry it.
    6. **The goal isn't zero risk, it's taking sensible risk intelligently.**

Point 2 matters especially, because it maps directly onto what the beginner section taught you: **keep buying in a downturn, because the more it falls, the higher the future expected return.** Most people's instinct is the exact opposite — fear on the way down, greed on the way up — and that is precisely why they lose money.

## 6. In practice: what is your portfolio actually exposed to?

With the framework in hand, let's get concrete. Here are a few common buy-and-hold portfolios viewed through Marks's lens. (**Note**: because these are all passive, low-cost, long-held ETFs, many risks don't really apply — credit risk (equities don't default), liquidity risk (ETFs trade easily), manager risk (passive, nobody is picking), leverage risk (assuming you haven't borrowed). So only the **genuinely relevant** ones are covered.)

=== "VOO (S&P 500, US large-cap)"

    - **Concentration risk**: weighted toward the US and toward mega-cap tech — concentrated by both sector and region.
    - **Valuation risk**: when US valuations run high for long stretches, the pressure to correct builds.
    - **Black swan / event risk**: a shock to one country drags everything down with it.
    - **Upside risk (missing out)**: betting only on the US means missing the years when international markets have their turn.
    - **In one line**: strong returns, but **a bet on a single country**, not diversified enough.

=== "VTI (whole US market)"

    - Similar to VOO, but **adding mid and small caps makes it slightly less concentrated and a little more diversified**.
    - Concentration risk and valuation risk are still **US systemic risk** — it's **still a bet on one country**.
    - **In one line**: a bit broader than VOO, but still inside the US.

=== "VT (global total market) ✅ this site's default"

    - **Regional concentration risk drops dramatically**: US + international + emerging markets in one holding.
    - What's left is mostly **market (systematic) risk** — the kind you **want, and are compensated for**, which can't be diversified away.
    - Volatility risk remains (a bad bear market still takes it down 30%+), as does purchasing-power risk (you're relying on equities to beat inflation long term).
    - **In one line**: it diversifies away the **uncompensated** risk of betting on which country wins, and leaves the risk that's worth carrying.

=== "60/40 stocks and bonds"

    - **Lower volatility than pure equity** (bonds cushion it), but **you take on interest-rate risk**: bonds fall when rates rise.
    - **Stocks and bonds can fall together**: in some periods (high inflation plus rate rises) both fall **at the same time** and the cushion stops working.
    - **Shortfall and purchasing-power risk rise**: long-run returns are usually lower than all-equity, and inflationary years are especially painful for bonds.
    - **In one line**: you buy smoothness with long-run return and inflation protection. Suits **conservative** investors or those **near retirement**.

!!! tip "Which one? Back to the age path"
    - Accumulating, long horizon → **VT** (diversify away the uncompensated concentration risk, carry the compensated market risk).
    - Near retirement, wanting less volatility → consider **adding some bonds** (a 60/40 kind of mix), understanding that you're trading return and inflation protection for smoothness.

## 7. Turning Marks's thinking into three actions

!!! success "These three are enough to take away"
    1. **Real risk = permanent loss, not volatility** → so: no leverage, no single stocks, no panic-selling in a downturn.
    2. **The future is a set of probabilities** → so: don't bet on "highest expected value", bet on "I survive the worst case" = diversify, hold emergency cash, never go all-in.
    3. **The more people feel there's no risk, the more dangerous it is** → so: be most careful when the market is most euphoric, and treat downturns as a friend.

---

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    1. Have you ever panic-sold because of volatility, only to watch the market recover? That was the "volatility = risk" myth at work.
    2. What's the largest single risk in your current portfolio? (Concentration? Interest rates? Purchasing power?) Are you comfortable with it?
    3. "The most dangerous moment is when everyone believes there's no risk" — do you sense that mood around you right now?

## Summary

- **Real risk = the possibility of permanent loss, not volatility.** Volatility can be ridden out; permanent loss can't be undone.
- **The future is a probability distribution** and can't be quantified precisely; don't bet on the highest expected value, bet on surviving the worst case.
- High risk ≠ guaranteed high return; it means **a wider range of outcomes** (including a worse tail).
- There are 24 kinds of risk, they conflict, and **reducing one usually raises another** (no free lunch).
- The counterintuitive truths: **safer as it falls, riskier as it rises; most dangerous when everyone feels safe.**
- In practice: VOO/VTI bet on one country (concentration risk); **VT diversifies away the uncompensated concentration risk**; 60/40 buys smoothness at the cost of return and inflation protection.
