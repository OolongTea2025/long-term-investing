# Chapter 7. Allocation path & leverage — lifecycle investing, mortgages, and why not LETFs

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 2–4 (accumulating to growing) · intermediate**<span class="lvtag">L2–L4</span>
    The beginner section told you to buy VT and sit still. This chapter goes a layer deeper: how should allocation change with age? What is this "leverage" everyone talks about, and is it worth using? Why do we tell you to stay away from LETFs, options and margin — while calling a mortgage on your own home the exception?

!!! tip "The short version"
    Young, long horizon, able to absorb volatility → **lean into equities**; from 30 → **VT for life, retirement included**; only if you know you'd panic in a crash should you consider a small bond cushion near retirement. On leverage: **we do not recommend LETFs, options or margin, full stop**; the only "leverage" worth using is **a mortgage on the home you live in, from Level 3 onward** — because it's cheap and it solves your housing need at the same time. For everything else, disciplined buying of VT is enough.

---

## 1. Allocation isn't about picking the best — it's about not having to predict

Start with a question:

> **Can you predict which asset will rise or fall next month?**

If you can, you don't need this chapter — you'd buy low, sell high, and take everything. But the fact is **nobody can** (the theory section proves this).

Precisely because the future can't be predicted, we need **asset allocation**. Its purpose is simple:

!!! note "The two goals of allocation (pick one)"
    1. **Same risk, higher long-run return**; or
    2. **Same return, lower volatility**.

    How: spread your money across **time, regions, assets and factors**. Don't bet on a single outcome, and no single outcome can destroy you.

## 2. The age path: from aggressive, gradually, to steady

Remember the age path from the [Level page](../level.md)? Here's the reasoning behind it.

| Stage | Rough allocation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Ages 0–20** | Lean toward VOO (US large-cap) | Very long horizon, able to take losses, chasing growth |
| **Ages 20–30** | Shift gradually from VOO to **VT** (the world) | Start diversifying regions, cut single-market risk |
| **From 30 on** | **VT** all the way, into old age (retirement included) | Globally diversified, rises long term, simple and reliable |

This path isn't a rigid rule that the young must be aggressive and the old must be conservative. The core logic is one sentence:

> **Concentrate on growth when young, move to global diversification, then leave it alone. Bonds are not a required stop — only if you're nearing retirement and know you'd panic in a crash should you add a little as a comfort cushion.**

As for whether you should hold 100% equities for life and never add bonds at all, even in retirement — that's a substantial academic argument in recent years, and the next chapter is devoted to it.

## 3. Diversifying across *time*: the lifecycle investing idea

Most people only think about diversifying across **regions and assets**, but there's a dimension that constantly gets overlooked — **time**.

Consider a very ordinary situation:

> At 25 you've just started working and have a few thousand. By 55 you might have accumulated a couple of million. Which means that **almost all of your lifetime exposure to the stock market is concentrated in the second half.**

And there's the problem: a big market fall in that second half damages **most of your lifetime wealth**; a big fall when you were young cost you a few thousand. **Risk is distributed extremely unevenly across time.**

The idea behind **lifecycle investing** is to **spread that risk out across time**:

!!! example "The original version (for understanding only — not a recommendation)"
    - **When young**: because the capital is small, even 100% equities, or a little leverage to "pre-buy" future exposure, only puts a modest dollar amount at risk.
    - **After middle age**: as capital grows, **reduce the leverage step by step and keep the dollar amount at risk roughly constant**.
    - **Approaching retirement**: gradually introduce bonds so that by the day you retire, equity risk is down to a level you chose in advance.

    Academically, rolling tests on US data from 1871–2009 found that the "diversified across time" cohort tended to end up with better lifetime wealth and risk characteristics than the "make one big bet in the second half of life" approach.

**The underlying idea is worth keeping**: rather than shouldering enormous risk all at once when you're older and wealthier, **spread the risk over a longer period** and give yourself more time to get used to market swings.

## 4. But — we're not telling you to use leverage

The "a little leverage when young" line above comes from the **original lifecycle investing papers**. In the real world we have to be blunt:

!!! danger "How hard leverage actually is"
    Lifecycle investing has one **very large practical problem**: what instrument is a young person actually supposed to use? There are only three roads — **margin, options, and leveraged ETFs (LETFs, the 2× and 3× kind)**. All three:

    - **Margin**: in a falling market you get margin-called and liquidated, forced to sell at the worst possible price → "temporary volatility" turns into "permanent loss".
    - **Options**: time decay, complexity, and it's easy to lose everything.
    - **LETFs**: **volatility decay** means holding long term isn't the same as "the index ×2"; a sideways market bleeds you slowly.

    Using these well requires **exceptional conviction, exceptional risk tolerance, and stable income and family circumstances**. The overwhelming majority of people can't manage it, and blow up the first time the market turns.

So this site's position is clear:

> **LETFs, options, margin — not recommended, any of them.**

What an ordinary person should actually do is **take the *idea* of lifecycle investing without the leverage**:

!!! tip "Lifecycle investing, the ordinary-person version"
    1. Start with **100% equity market exposure** (VT, for example) and stay in the market long term.
    2. Once your savings reach a certain share of your projected lifetime wealth, **stop adding exposure** and simply stay in VT. (If you want a cushion near retirement, that's when to consider a little in bonds — see Chapter 14.)
    3. Even with no leverage at all, you capture most of the benefit of "diversifying across time".

    Put differently: **you don't need leverage to get the essence of lifecycle investing.**

## 5. The new leverage: return-stacking (RSSB / NTSX) and managed futures — worth it?

LETFs, options and margin are "old-school" leverage. Recent years have brought a wave of **capital-efficiency products** whose pitch sounds far cleverer, and readers ask about them constantly — so here's a section on them.

!!! note "What problem are they trying to solve?"
    These products (commonly marketed as **return stacking** or **capital efficiency**, typically stacking equities and bonds, sometimes with a managed futures / CTA layer on top) rest on one core idea: **getting more than \$1 of exposure from \$1**.

    For example: the same pile of money **simultaneously** holds a global equity sleeve and a bond sleeve (and possibly a trend-following layer as well). On the face of it you've given up no equity exposure and gained a "free" extra layer of diversification — and in backtests these often beat a traditional 60/40. Unlike an LETF, this isn't simply "the index ×2"; it's **stacking different assets**.

That sounds appealing, so why do we still **not recommend holding it long term**? Because underneath the packaging it is **still leverage**, just better dressed. The costs of leverage don't go away:

!!! danger "Three costs you can't escape"
    - **Borrowing cost**: that exposure is borrowed via futures, swaps or repo, and it carries interest. **When rates are high, that cost quietly eats the return of the extra layer** — it is not a free lunch.
    - **You take on more interest-rate risk, and may not be paid for it**: the stacked bond layer adds duration and interest-rate risk. But the **term premium can be very low for long stretches, even negative** — meaning you carry more risk without the market necessarily paying you for it.
    - **Complexity + fees + hidden friction**: management fees, swap costs and futures roll costs stack up. **The more complex a product is, the harder it is to know what you're actually carrying and what you're actually paying.**

!!! tip "The more fundamental layer: you don't need it"
    As Chapter 8 will cover, the most recent long-horizon research (Cederburg and colleagues) finds that **100% global equity on its own is already good enough over the long run (20–30 years and beyond).** The long-run return you're after is something pure equity already gives you — **more simply, more transparently, and more cheaply**.

    Bolting a leveraged stock-and-bond layer on top of that buys you **more risk, more cost and more difficulty understanding it**, without necessarily buying a better outcome. This is **the same argument as "don't use LETFs"**: don't add complexity you can't hold onto in a downturn just because it sounds cleverer.

> **In one line**: the capital-efficiency pitch is understandable, but once you add up cost, risk and complexity it isn't worth it long term. Back to the simplest sentence — **VT, or an equivalent global total-market ETF, is enough.**

## 6. The one exception: a mortgage on the home you live in

So why does this chapter say "no leverage" throughout, and then call a home mortgage the exception? Because it is fundamentally different from the three speculative kinds above.

!!! note "Why a home mortgage is 'good leverage'"
    - **Extremely cheap**: mortgage rates are normally far below any other borrowing, and it is the cheapest money an ordinary person will ever be lent.
    - **You don't get liquidated**: as long as you keep up the payments, the bank won't force you to repay because prices dipped — there is no margin call.
    - **It solves your housing need at the same time**: you have to live somewhere. Borrowing to buy the home you live in **solves "somewhere to live" and "cheap leveraged participation in an appreciating asset" in one move**.
    - **The repayments are themselves diversification across time**: each instalment is similar in size, so early and late money go in at comparable amounts, which naturally avoids the over-exposure of a single lump-sum bet.

    Consider: a 30% deposit already means roughly **3.33× leverage** — but for the reasons above it's one of the few forms of leverage an ordinary person can safely carry.

!!! warning "But be clear about the cost"
    Buying the home you live in means putting a large sum, **all at one moment**, into **a single asset in a single location — the city you live in**. You **give up the benefit of being diversified across assets, factors and regions at that same moment**.

    So a home is "a housing need plus cheap leverage", not "the optimal investment". Treat it as a decision for **after Level 3 (investable net worth $100k–$1M)** — and don't drain every investable asset and your emergency fund to get on the ladder. Deposit sizes, rates and terms differ enormously by country, so check what applies where you live.

## 7. Rebalancing: maintenance for your allocation

Setting your target allocation doesn't settle it forever. Over time, assets move differently and the proportions **drift**.

> Example: you started at equities : bonds = 80 : 20. After a strong run in stocks it's 88 : 12 — your portfolio has quietly become **more aggressive**.

**Rebalancing** = periodically (say once a year) **moving the proportions back to the targets you originally set**.

Why does it help?

- **Automatic "sell high, buy low"**: trim what ran up, top up what fell — exactly the correct move, and exactly the one most people can't make.
- **Risk control**: it stops your portfolio drifting steadily into something more dangerous.

!!! tip "Rebalancing isn't a way to make quick money"
    Some people think rebalancing lets you beat the market. Its main function is **risk control**, not generating excess return. For rebalancing itself to earn money, assets would have to mean-revert strongly against each other, and that doesn't reliably hold. **Treat it as maintenance, not a money printer.**

    Also: **if you're in the "one VT" camp you barely need to rebalance at all**, because VT already adjusts its global weights by market cap internally.

## 8. The mindset: not the best allocation, the one you can hold

One last idea, and possibly the most important:

!!! quote "What allocation is really about"
    **Finding an allocation that does reasonably well in a range of conditions, and then sticking to it, matters more than finding the theoretically optimal one.**

    Because the "best in hindsight" allocation is only knowable in hindsight, whereas an allocation you **can actually hold, and won't panic out of in a downturn**, is the one that gets you to the finish line.

## Summary

- The purpose of allocation: **because the future can't be predicted**, spread across time, regions, assets and factors.
- The age path: lean into equities when young (VOO→VT), then VT from 30 onward, retirement included; if you'd panic in a crash, consider a small bond cushion near retirement.
- **Lifecycle investing** teaches diversification across time — but an ordinary person **captures its essence without any leverage**.
- **LETFs, options, margin: not recommended** (liquidation, time decay, volatility decay).
- **The new "capital efficiency" products (return stacking, stacked stocks-and-bonds, managed futures)**: better packaging, same leverage underneath — borrowing cost, extra interest-rate risk, complexity and fees make them a poor long-run deal. Plain VT is enough.
- **The only leverage worth using = a mortgage on your own home from Level 3 onward** (cheap, no liquidation, solves housing), accepting the cost of concentration in one asset and one location.
- **Rebalancing** is maintenance (risk control), not a money printer; the "one VT" camp barely needs it.
- Mindset: an allocation you **can hold** beats one that's theoretically optimal.
