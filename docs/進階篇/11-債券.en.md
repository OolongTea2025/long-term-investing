# Chapter 11. Bonds, the full guide — duration, default, corporate or government?

!!! who "Who this chapter is for"
    **Level 2–5 · intermediate**<span class="lvtag">L2–L5</span>
    After all that talk of equities, it's time to get clear on bonds. What drives bond returns? Why do we only recommend taking duration risk and steer clear of corporate bonds? Which is better, corporates or governments? Is going all-in on long bonds a guaranteed win? This chapter settles it.

!!! tip "The short version"
    Bond returns are driven by exactly **two risks**: **term (duration) risk** and **credit (default) risk**. Only **term risk is worth carrying** (it's compensated over the long run); **credit risk pays a small premium and is highly correlated with equities, so it doesn't diversify — a bad deal.** So: **intermediate US Treasuries (IEF or VGIT) are enough for your bond sleeve, you don't need corporate bonds, and you certainly shouldn't fantasise about going all-in on long bonds.**

---

## 1. Bond returns are determined by just two risks

Equities have five factors; bonds are much simpler — **there are only two**:

!!! note "Bond asset pricing: two factors"
    1. **Term risk**: the **longer** you lend for, the more uncertainty you take on (especially from interest-rate movements), so you should be paid a premium.
    2. **Credit risk**: how likely is the borrower to fail to repay principal or interest? The more likely a default, the more interest you should be paid.

    One key assumption: **US Treasuries are assumed not to default** → their credit risk ≈ 0 → they can be treated as the **risk-free rate**.

That lets us **separate the two risks** and see which one is worth carrying.

---

## 2. Term risk: the one worth carrying

How do you measure the **term premium**? Take the return on long-dated government bonds and subtract the return on very short-dated ones (bills):

!!! example "The term premium (rough historical figure)"
    **Term premium ≈ 20-year Treasury yield − 1-month Treasury yield ≈ around 2.5%** (a rough estimate over the 1927–2015 period)

    A few properties:
    - **Durable**: it shows up broadly across different markets and periods.
    - **Extremely low trading cost**: US Treasuries are one of the most liquid and cheapest markets in the world.
    - **Negatively correlated with equity factors**: this matters a lot — **the term factor tends to move in the opposite direction to equity factors**, so including it in a portfolio **diversifies risk** (bonds often hold up when stocks fall).

    (Note: its Sharpe ratio is only slightly better than the size factor's, so don't mistake it for easy money. Its value is mostly in **diversification**, not in high returns.)

**Conclusion: term risk is worth carrying.** That's where the return and the diversification of your bond sleeve come from.

---

## 3. Credit risk: the one that isn't worth carrying

So what about corporate bonds? They carry **default risk** on top of government bonds, so they should pay more interest — and that extra bit is the **credit premium**:

!!! warning "The credit premium (rough historical figure) — too small to be worth it"
    **Credit premium ≈ return on 20-year investment-grade corporate bonds − same-maturity Treasury yield ≈ around 0.3%**

    And that 0.3%:
    - **Is before trading costs** (corporate bonds cost more to trade than Treasuries).
    - **Is before fund fees** (corporate bond funds usually charge more).

    Once you subtract those, **you've taken on a large slab of default risk and may have almost nothing left to show for it.** Statistically: **carrying credit risk does not reliably bring good returns — high risk does not equal high return.** (Which echoes Marks in Chapter 9.)

The more damaging point is next.

---

## 4. Corporates vs governments: it isn't only about return, it's about correlation

Even if corporate bonds return slightly more, they have a **fatal weakness**:

!!! danger "Corporate bonds are highly correlated with equities → they don't diversify"
    - **Corporate bonds are highly correlated with the US stock market**: when stocks fall, corporates often **fall with them** (a weak economy → companies get into trouble → default risk rises).
    - **Government bonds have low correlation with equities**: in turbulent markets, Treasuries often **hold up or even rise** (safe-haven flows).

    Which means: you bought bonds wanting a cushion that holds up when stocks fall, and corporate bonds **collapse alongside equities at exactly the moment you need them** — completely defeating the purpose.

Research (*Explaining the Rate Spread on Corporate Bonds*) even shows that part of the corporate bond spread can be explained by **equity market risk premia (the Fama–French factors)** — meaning **corporate bond returns contain a lump of systematic risk you already own through equities, which you cannot diversify away.**

!!! quote "This site's position"
    **We don't recommend adding corporate bonds to your allocation.** On a risk-adjusted basis (Sortino ratio, for example) corporates lose to governments: slightly higher return, considerably more volatility and risk.

    **If you want to take risk in pursuit of return, take it with equities (VT); if you want a steady cushion, take it with government bonds. Corporate bonds do neither job well.**

---

## 5. In practice: what should your bond sleeve be?

!!! tip "The simple answer: intermediate US Treasuries"
    - Buy an **intermediate US Treasury ETF and hold it long term** — **IEF** or **VGIT**.
    - The main difference between the two is their **yield to maturity (YTM)**; pick on preference.
    - Why intermediate? It balances **term premium** against **interest-rate volatility** — you get paid for term risk without the extreme swings of long bonds.

    **In one line: the bond sleeve = intermediate Treasuries, no corporate bonds, simple and direct.**

!!! note "Why hold to maturity?"
    If you **hold a bond to maturity**, what you lock in is the **yield to maturity (YTM)** at the moment you bought it. If your aim is to trade bond prices (buy low, sell high), that's **a different objective and a different game**, and it isn't part of long-term allocation.

---

## 6. Busting a myth: "going all-in on long bonds is a sure thing"

Near the end of every rate-hiking cycle, someone says "go all-in on long bonds now, wait for cuts, prices will surge". There are several traps in that:

!!! danger "Long bonds are not a sure thing"
    1. **Long and short rates don't move in lockstep**: when the central bank cuts the short-term policy rate, **long bond yields don't necessarily follow immediately**. What you're actually betting on is long rates, not short ones.
    2. **Short-term returns are extremely volatile and can be negative**: buy a 10-year Treasury at a given yield and the return a year later is **highly variable and can easily be negative**. Making money on it within a year **is not a given**.
    3. **Long bonds only deliver that YTM if you hold to maturity**; trying to capture price gains is betting on the direction of rates — and rate direction is **as hard to predict as market timing in equities**.

!!! warning "The bigger trap: wrecking your whole allocation to trade bonds"
    If you **suddenly raise your bond weighting because you're bullish on bonds**, you've **changed the risk level of your entire allocation**, and you may **miss the equity recovery** as well.

    > Remember Chapter 7: **once you've set your allocation, don't adjust it on a whim.** Breaking your allocation discipline to chase an asset you think will rise is exactly the behaviour this whole site tells you to avoid.

---

## 7. The correct role of bonds in your portfolio

!!! success "Bonds are the cushion, not the star"
    - **Accumulation phase (young to middle-aged)**: you can hold **no bonds or very few**, staying all-equity (VT) for long-run growth (see Chapters 7 and 8).
    - **Approaching or in retirement**: add a slice of **intermediate Treasuries**, whose job is to **reduce volatility, hold your portfolio up in a severe equity fall, and cut sequence risk** (the Retirement section covers this).
    - **Taking risk in pursuit of return**: that's the equities' job. **Bonds are not there to chase returns**, they're there so you can sleep and stay invested.

## Summary

- Bond returns are driven by two factors: **term risk** and **credit risk**.
- **Term risk is worth carrying**: a premium of around 2.5%, low costs, and **negative correlation** with equities (genuine diversification value).
- **Credit risk isn't worth carrying**: a premium of only around 0.3% before costs and fees, and **highly correlated with equities, so it doesn't diversify**.
- **Don't add corporate bonds**: worse risk-adjusted than governments, and they fall alongside equities exactly when you need them most.
- In practice: **intermediate US Treasuries (IEF / VGIT)**, held long term, are enough for the bond sleeve.
- **"All-in on long bonds is a sure thing" is a myth**: long and short rates diverge, short-term swings are large, and it wrecks allocation discipline.
- Bonds are a **cushion (lower volatility, less sequence risk)**, not the vehicle for chasing returns.
