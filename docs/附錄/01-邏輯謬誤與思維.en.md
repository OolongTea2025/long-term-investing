# Appendix A. Logical fallacies & thinking tools — how to see through what "investing experts" tell you

!!! who "Who this is for"
    **All Levels · dip in any time**<span class="lvtag">L1–L5</span>
    Chapter 6 covered **the traps inside your own head** (emotional biases). This covers the other half: **how to judge whether someone teaching you about investing online, on a forum or on YouTube is actually making sense.** Read both and you have defences pointing inward and outward.

!!! tip "The short version"
    The one thing the investing world is never short of is **nonsense that sounds reasonable**. Learn a few common logical fallacies and you stop being taken in by "I told you so" and "even so-and-so says this". **Judge a claim by its evidence and logic, not by how loudly or confidently it's delivered.**

---

## 1. Five traps in what other people say

These five aren't emotional problems — they're **defects in the argument itself**. Learn to spot them and you'll save a lot of money.

### 1. Appeal to authority

> "Even a senior official / a big bank's analyst says it's going up — you still don't believe it?"

Using the speaker's status as evidence that the claim is correct. But **status ≠ correctness**. The classic example: officials saying "inflation is transitory, no need to raise rates" — an impeccably authoritative source, proved wrong afterwards.

!!! note "The counter"
    The question isn't **who said it** but **what evidence they have and whether the logic holds up**. Authority can be a **starting point** (a prompt to go and check), never an **end point** (a reason to just believe).

### 2. Ad hominem

> "You've only got a few thousand to your name — what gives you the right to talk about investing?"

Instead of rebutting **what was said**, attacking **the person** (their wealth, their credentials, their tone). Whether a claim is correct has **nothing to do with who made it**.

!!! tip "A useful tool: Graham's hierarchy of disagreement"
    Paul Graham sorted disagreement into seven levels, worst to best: **name-calling → responding to tone → ad hominem → contradiction without evidence → counterargument → refutation of the central point → refuting the other side's core claim directly**. Next time you see someone "rebutting" something, notice which level they're on. **Anything stuck at the bottom levels can simply be skipped.**

### 3. Correlation ≠ causation

Two things happening together (correlation) doesn't mean one caused the other (causation).

> "It went up after I bought it → so my judgement is sound." — it may have been pure luck.
> "This strategy beat the market for ten years → so it works." — it may just have coincided with the right market.

!!! warning "A disaster area in investing"
    Almost every "magic indicator" and "guaranteed pattern" dies here: **finding a pattern that correlated in the past, then treating it as causal and assuming it will keep working.** Real causation requires a mechanism *and* has to survive different periods and different markets (which is exactly what the whole optional deep-dive section is about).

### 4. Survivorship bias

You only see **the ones that succeeded**, never **the ones that failed and disappeared**.

> "I know someone who got rich picking stocks" — you see the one who got rich; the people who bet just as heavily and lost everything **stopped talking about it long ago**, so you never see them.

!!! example "Why it's so dangerous"
    Get-rich-by-trading stories are loud and widely repeated; the people who lost everything leave quietly. So you **overestimate the success rate of concentrated bets**. The same applies to "which fund manager wins year after year" and "which stock went up tenfold in a decade" — remember you're looking at **the survivors**, not the whole population.

### 5. Hasty generalisation / too small a sample

Using **one or two examples** to establish a **general rule**. "My friend got rich flipping property, so flipping property works" — one person over one period proves nothing. Conclusions about long-term investing have to rest on data with **large samples, long periods and multiple markets**, not two or three anecdotes.

## 2. Four thinking tools that actually help

Once you can dismantle fallacies, it's worth installing a few constructive tools.

### 1. DIKW: climbing from data to wisdom

!!! note "Data → information → knowledge → wisdom"
    - **Data**: VT fell 2% today. (Raw numbers.)
    - **Information**: global markets are pulling back this month. (Organised, with context.)
    - **Knowledge**: historically, pullbacks are normal and the long-run trend is up. (Seeing the pattern.)
    - **Wisdom**: so I do nothing and keep buying on schedule. (Knowing **what to do**.)

    Most people are **stuck at data and information**, chasing news and watching intraday prices while their emotions get dragged around. What's actually useful is climbing to **knowledge and wisdom** — and those top two levels are exactly where this site has been trying to take you from the beginning.

### 2. Probabilistic thinking: think expected value, not a single outcome

The future isn't one definite outcome, it's **a probability distribution** (as Chapter 9 covered on risk). Instead of asking "will it go up?", ask "**what are the probabilities and consequences of the various cases, and is the weighted result worth it?**" You buy VT not because you're certain it will rise, but because **over the long run both the odds and the expected value are on your side**.

### 3. Invert: not "how do I win" but "how do I not get wiped out"

Investing isn't a bet on maximising the best case, it's making sure **you survive the worst case** (echoing Howard Marks in Chapter 9). Before you open any position, ask: **if I'm wrong, how bad does this get, and can I take it?** Only proceed if you can.

### 4. Mean reversion: nothing grows to the sky, nothing shrinks to nothing

Extremes tend to drift back toward the middle. Runaway themes and strategies that outperform absurdly all feel the pull of **reversion to the mean** eventually. So when you see something that's been spectacular for five years, don't assume it stays spectacular — this is one of the underlying reasons for not chasing whatever's hot.

!!! warning "But don't abuse it in reverse"
    "It's fallen a lot so it must bounce" is a **misuse** of mean reversion (= the gambler's fallacy, see Chapter 6). Mean reversion describes **a long-run directional pull**, not a short-term timing tool that says "it's fallen enough, it'll bounce now".

!!! question "Something to think about 🤔"
    1. The last time you made an investing decision because "a guru or influencer said so", what **evidence** did they actually give you?
    2. Is the "sure thing" you believe in really just survivorship bias plus correlation mistaken for causation?
    3. Do you normally consume "data and information", or do you actually get to "knowledge and wisdom"?

## Summary

- Chapter 6 defends against what's **inside** (your own emotional biases); this defends against what's **outside** (holes in other people's arguments).
- Five fallacies to watch for: **appeal to authority, ad hominem, correlation as causation, survivorship bias, hasty generalisation**.
- Judge a claim by its **evidence and logic**, not by **status and volume**.
- Four thinking tools: **DIKW, probability and expected value, thinking about the worst case first, mean reversion**.
- One thing to remember: **the more certain and the more simply enriching something sounds, the more careful you should be.**
